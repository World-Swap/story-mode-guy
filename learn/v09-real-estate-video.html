A listing with a smooth, bright video tour gets more attention than one with a phone-snapshot slideshow, and the person filming it can get paid. Property video is one of the more learnable video jobs: the subject holds still, the lighting is fixed, and nobody is running. Your job is to move a steady camera through a space in a logical order and expose it well.

This covers how to film a real estate or Airbnb walkthrough from the settings to the edit: the lens and camera settings for interiors, how to handle bright windows, the order a buyer expects to see a home, and how to move through rooms without bounce. Whether you are filming your own listing or planning to charge agents and hosts, the technical craft is the same.

Who it's for

Side-hustlers, agents, and hosts filming their own listings. A listing with a smooth, bright video tour books more showings and gets more Airbnb clicks than one with a phone-snapshot slideshow — and the person who films it gets paid. You already know how to walk through a house. This guide teaches you to film that walk so it looks professional, sells the property, and earns you money doing it.

What camera settings should I use for a property walkthrough?

Shoot rooms at a full-frame-equivalent 16 to 24mm. Wider bows the walls and makes a bedroom look cartoonishly large, which leaves buyers feeling deceived at the showing. Narrower makes a normal room feel like a closet. Film at 24 or 30fps with the shutter at about double the frame rate, 1/50 or 1/60. Keep ISO low, roughly 100 to 800, and use an aperture around f/5.6 so a whole room stays sharp.

Lock things before you walk in: manual exposure so brightness does not jump between rooms, manual white balance around 5000K held for the entire shoot, and focus locked rather than hunting mid-move. Turn image stabilization on for handheld or gimbal and off on a tripod. If you buy one stabilizer, make it a gimbal. Shoot detail shots like a curtain moving or a coffee pour at 60fps and slow them down for a premium feel.

How do I film rooms with bright windows?

Windows are the hardest exposure problem in interiors, because the outdoors can be many stops brighter than the room and the camera cannot hold both. In most tours the room is the subject, so expose for the interior and let windows go gently bright. A pure white rectangle looks careless; pull exposure down until you see a hint of what is outside, then lift the room with lights.

The cheapest fix is turning on every lamp, overhead, and under-cabinet light, which narrows the gap. Shoot on a bright overcast day or near golden hour instead of harsh midday sun. When the view is the selling point, such as an ocean or skyline, expose for the window and add light inside. Shooting in a flat or log profile gives you more highlight room to balance in the edit. Keep white balance fixed so rooms do not flicker between orange and blue.

What order should I shoot a house tour in?

Follow the order a visitor would experience it: approach from the street, entry and foyer, living spaces ending in the kitchen, the primary bedroom first, then other bedrooms, bathrooms, and finally the outdoor amenity that sells the lifestyle. Within each room, shoot a wide establishing shot to show shape and size, then a detail of the reason to want the room, such as the fireplace, counters, or the view. If the kitchen or view is the headline, give it more time.

Film the doorway transitions too. The signature move is the doorway reveal, gliding through a threshold so the room opens up, and it lets you cut one continuous walk. Before filming, declutter, make beds, clear counters, and check mirrors for your own reflection. Edit clips to 2 to 4 seconds, cut on the music beat, and export a 9:16 social cut under 45 seconds alongside the horizontal tour.

Common questions

What lens is best for real estate video?

Aim for a full-frame-equivalent 16 to 24mm, which is roughly 10 to 16mm on a crop sensor. That shows the room without stretching it. Ultra-wide lenses bend vertical lines and make rooms look bigger than they are, which can disappoint buyers when they visit.

Should I use a gimbal or tripod for property tours?

Use both. A gimbal gives the smooth moving shots that separate paid work from home video, while a tripod or levelled monopod handles locked-off establishing frames. If you can only buy one, buy the gimbal. A phone gimbal works for vertical or social clips.

How long should a real estate video be?

It depends on the destination. A full MLS-style tour runs around 60 to 120 seconds, an Airbnb or VRBO listing 45 to 90 seconds, and an Instagram Reel or TikTok 15 to 45 seconds in vertical with a hook in the first second.

Do I need a license to use a drone for property video?

Flying for a client is commercial use, which in most countries requires a license or registration. In the US that is the FAA Part 107 certificate. You also need to respect no-fly zones and privacy rules. Drone shots are optional, so skip them until you meet your local requirements.
