If you are not sure whether your drone flight is legal, you are asking the right question. In the United States, the rules mostly come down to two goals: keep drones from hitting aircraft, and keep them from hurting people on the ground. Once you see them that way, drone laws, registration, and Part 107 stop feeling like red tape and start looking like a checklist you can run before each flight.

This page walks through the US FAA framework: whether your flight counts as recreational or commercial, when registration is needed, how to check airspace, and what the Part 107 certificate is for. It is educational information, not legal advice. Rules change and vary by country, state, and even city park, so always verify the current requirements with the FAA, or your national aviation authority if you fly outside the US, before you fly.

Who it's for

Pilots unsure what's legal, and anyone who wants to fly commercially. Almost every rule that governs your drone was written for one of two reasons: to keep it from hitting an airplane, or to keep it from hurting someone on the ground. Once you understand the system that way, the whole thing stops feeling like bureaucracy and starts feeling like a checklist you can actually run — and the same checklist that keeps you legal is the one that lets you charge money to fly.

Do I need Part 107 or just TRUST to fly my drone?

In the US system, the purpose of the flight decides which rules apply, not the size of the drone or the quality of the footage. Flying purely for personal enjoyment falls under the recreational exception, which asks you to pass a free online safety test called TRUST and carry your certificate. Flying for compensation, or to further a business, is commercial and falls under Part 107, which requires the Remote Pilot Certificate from a proctored knowledge exam.

The FAA reads compensation broadly. Shooting a friend's shop opening for free still furthers a business, and so can trading footage for product or monetizing a YouTube channel. When you are unsure, the safe approach is to treat the flight as commercial. TRUST is not a pilot's license, and passing it does not authorize a single paid flight. Confirm the current definitions and requirements on the FAA website before you decide which path fits you.

Do I have to register my drone with the FAA?

The number to remember is 250 grams, about 0.55 lb. For recreational flying, a drone under that takeoff weight generally does not need registration, while one at or above it must be registered before outdoor flights. Commercial flying under Part 107 removes the weight break, so even a tiny sub-250g drone has to be registered if you fly it for work.

Weigh the drone exactly as it flies, with battery, prop guards, and any accessory attached. A drone that is 249g bare can cross the line once you add guards or a larger battery. Registration is done on the official FAA DroneZone site for a small fee, the number must be readable on the outside of the aircraft, and you should carry proof while flying. Most registered drones also need to broadcast Remote ID, either built in or through an add-on module, so check what your model supports. Fees and details can change, so confirm on the FAA site.

How do I check where I can legally fly a drone?

The sky is divided into layers of airspace. Uncontrolled Class G airspace covers most rural and suburban ground away from airports, and flying there is generally allowed up to 400 feet above ground level, still following all other rules. Controlled airspace (Classes B, C, D, and E) surrounds airports, and you need authorization before flying there. LAANC, the Low Altitude Authorization and Notification Capability, can grant that authorization quickly through FAA-approved apps, up to the altitude ceiling shown on a grid over each airport.

Do not guess from the ground. A small regional airport can put controlled airspace over a quiet town, while a dense city block may sit in open Class G. Temporary Flight Restrictions can appear over stadiums, wildfires, or VIP movements on the same day you planned to fly. Check an official or approved airspace app at your actual launch spot on the day, and verify the current rules with the FAA before flying.

Common questions

What is the difference between TRUST and Part 107?

TRUST is a free, online safety test for recreational flyers, and it cannot be failed. Part 107 is the Remote Pilot Certificate, earned by passing a proctored aeronautical knowledge exam, and it is required for flights that are compensated or further a business. Verify current requirements with the FAA.

Can I fly my drone over people or cars?

Generally no. The core rules say not to fly over people who are not part of your operation or over moving vehicles unless you specifically meet the conditions that permit it, which depend on your drone category and safety features. Check the current FAA rules before any flight near crowds or roads.

Is 400 feet measured from where I launched?

No. The 400 foot limit in uncontrolled airspace is above ground level, measured from the ground directly beneath the drone. Flying toward a hill or cliff, the terrain can rise so you exceed 400 feet AGL even if your app shows a lower climb from takeoff. Controlled airspace ceilings can be lower.

Is this page legal advice?

No. It is an educational overview of US FAA fundamentals as commonly understood. Regulations are updated often and differ by country, state, and local park rules. Before every flight, verify the current requirements with the FAA, or your own national aviation authority if you fly outside the United States.
