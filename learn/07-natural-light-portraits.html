If you are trying to figure out how to take better portraits with natural light, start by ignoring your gear. Portraits come down to two things: where you stand your subject, and whether they feel at ease. A phone in portrait mode, used in good light with a relaxed subject, will beat an expensive camera used carelessly.

The frustrating part is that most flattering-light advice stops at "shoot at golden hour." Real sessions happen at noon, under trees, and in kitchens with ugly ceiling bulbs. Below are the practical moves that work in those conditions, plus the lens, aperture, and posing choices that make faces look their best.

Who it's for

Hobbyists photographing family, friends, and clients. The photographers whose portraits make you stop scrolling are not shooting better cameras than you. They are standing their subject in better light and making that subject feel at ease. Master those two things — where you put a person and how you make them feel — and a phone in portrait mode will out-shoot a $3,000 camera used carelessly. This guide is entirely about those two things.

What is the best natural light for portraits?

Soft, directional light. The easiest source is open shade: the shadowed area beside a building, under an awning, or on the shady side of a street, where your subject faces big open sky that acts like a giant softbox. An overcast day does the same thing everywhere, though flat gray light can look lifeless, so stand your subject near a doorway or wall that blocks light from one side to restore shape.

A large window with no direct sun is a studio you already own. Turn the room lights off, place your subject at about 45 degrees to the glass, two to four feet away, and bounce fill from a white foam board on the shadow side. Golden hour, the hour after sunrise and before sunset, gives low, warm, flattering light. Check the eyes for catchlights, the small reflections that make a face look alive.

How do I fix harsh midday sun or dappled shade in portraits?

Move the subject rather than fighting the light. For harsh midday sun, walk them into open shade. If there is none, turn them so the sun hits the back of their head and expose for the shaded face. That trades squinting and raccoon-eye shadows for a soft backlit look. For backlight, spot meter on the cheek or add +1 to +2 stops of exposure compensation, and let the background go bright.

Dappled light from tree leaves leaves blotches across the face. The messy zone is the edge of the tree's shadow, so walk your subject 5 to 10 feet deeper into solid shade, or fully out of it. Indoors, turn off overhead bulbs and use a window instead, since ceiling light digs shadows into the eye sockets. A collapsible 5-in-1 reflector costs under $25 and bounces fill, softens sun, and shades your lens.

What lens and aperture should I use for flattering portraits?

Focal length changes the shape of a face. Wide lenses under about 35mm exaggerate whatever is closest to the camera, so a close headshot makes the nose balloon. Use 50mm or longer and back up. 85mm is the classic head-and-shoulders lens, and 105 to 135mm gives maximum flattery and background blur. On a phone, use the 2x or 3x telephoto lens instead of the main wide camera.

For aperture, f/1.8 to f/2.8 melts the background. Wide open, the sharp zone can be thinner than a face, so focus on the near eye. Use f/2.8 to f/4 for two people and f/5.6 or more for a group of five. Keep your shutter at 1/125s or faster for posed portraits and 1/250s with kids. If light drops, raise ISO before you let the shutter slow, because a little grain beats a soft face.

Common questions

Why do my portraits look flat or unflattering outdoors?

Usually the light is hitting the face from the wrong direction or is too hard. Move the subject into open shade, or turn them so light comes from the side and slightly in front. Turning the face slightly away so the shadow falls on the side nearer the camera slims it.

What is the best focal length for portraits?

85mm is the classic choice for head-and-shoulders portraits, with 50mm good for half and full-length shots. Longer lenses compress features flatteringly, while anything under about 35mm distorts a face if you shoot close. Back up and use 50mm or longer.

How do I get my subject to relax in front of the camera?

Give specific physical directions instead of saying "be natural," such as turning shoulders or shifting weight to the back foot. Keep talking, ask questions about their life, and shoot during the conversation. Asking for a deliberately fake laugh often tips into a real one a couple of seconds later.

How do I expose a backlit portrait so the face isn't dark?

Meter off the face, not the sky. Use spot metering on the cheek, or add +1 to +2 stops of exposure compensation. On a phone, tap the face and drag the exposure up. A bright, slightly glowing background is the look, and a reflector can lift the shaded face.
