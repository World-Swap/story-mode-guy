Log, LUTs & the Filmic Look — Without the Guesswork

You switched to log, dropped a LUT on the clip, and now it looks grey, green or worse than before. That is the classic first week of color grading, and it is not your camera or your eye. It is that color happens in two stages, correction and then grading, and most tutorials skip the first.

If you are searching for how to color grade log footage, the fix is to measure the image rather than guess. Scopes show what your eyes and an uncalibrated laptop screen hide. The three sections below cover reading scopes, handling log and LUTs in the right order, and building a base look that keeps skin looking human.

Who it's for

Shooters whose footage looks flat, grey, or inconsistent. You bought a camera that shoots log, watched a tutorial that promised "cinematic," dragged a LUT onto your clip, and it still looks flat, grey, or weirdly green. The problem isn't your camera and it isn't your eye — it's that nobody told you color happens in two separate stages, in a fixed order, using tools that measure the image instead of guessing at it. This guide fixes that.

How do I read the waveform, parade and vectorscope?

Your eyes adapt to a color cast within about thirty seconds, so use the scopes. The waveform maps brightness: bottom is black, top is white. Aim for blacks around 5 to 10 IRE, skin and midtones around 50 to 70, and highlights topping out near 90 to 100, with clipping reserved for light sources. Do not slam blacks to zero, because you lose shadow detail you cannot recover.

The RGB parade shows red, green and blue side by side. On a white wall or grey road the three should line up, and if blue sits higher in the shadows you have a blue cast. The vectorscope shows hue and saturation, and it includes a skin-tone line along which correctly balanced skin of every complexion falls. Keep the parade open permanently. Balance Lift, Gamma and Gain separately in Resolve to align the channels in shadows, mids and highlights, which cleans up mixed lighting.

How do I use LUTs on log footage without it looking wrong?

There are two kinds of LUT and mixing them up causes most of the frustration. A conversion LUT, such as S-Log3 to Rec.709, undoes your camera's log curve and must match your exact camera and profile. A look LUT adds a creative mood and expects a normal image beneath it. Dropping a look LUT onto raw log forces it to style a flat, lifted, desaturated image, which gives muddy shadows and green skin.

The order is: convert to Rec.709 (or use Resolve's Color Space Transform or project color management), correct exposure and white balance shot by shot, and only then grade or apply a look. Even then, pull the LUT's intensity back to roughly 40 to 70 percent, since look LUTs are nearly always too strong at full. Treat a LUT as a starting point, not a finished grade, and keep each job on its own node so you can fix one stage without untangling the rest.

How do I build a natural-looking base grade and fix skin tones?

Once the image is neutral, shape it with Lift for shadows, Gamma for midtones and Gain for highlights. A safe recipe for people and daylight is a faintly cool Lift, neutral to slightly warm Gamma, slightly warm Gain, and saturation up by 5 to 15. You should barely see the wheels leave center. Add contrast with a gentle S-curve rather than a contrast slider, and let highlights roll off instead of hitting a wall, since that soft shoulder is much of the filmic feel.

Check faces on the vectorscope. If the skin trace drifts off the skin-tone line, people look unwell. Faintly green faces are the usual log tell, and removing a little green from the midtones, which means adding magenta, often snaps skin back. Do not fix skin by piling on orange, and go easy on the orange and teal split. Cutting it by half usually reads as taste.

Common questions

What is the difference between color correction and color grading?

Correction makes the image accurate: neutral exposure, correct white balance, proper black and white points and believable skin. Grading adds the creative mood on top. Always correct first, because a look is designed to sit on a normal image, and the same LUT behaves very differently on corrected versus uncorrected footage.

Why does my footage look washed out after I upload it?

Usually a color space mismatch or a missed conversion. Grade, monitor and export in Rec.709 for normal web video, and make sure your log-to-Rec.709 step is active on the render, not just in the viewer. Also use a healthy bitrate to avoid banding, and test on a phone before publishing.

Do I need to shoot in log to color grade?

No. Log gives the most latitude but needs careful exposure and a mandatory grade. If you will not grade, a standard or flat profile is a better choice. A flat profile sits between the two, giving more room than standard with less hassle than log.

Is DaVinci Resolve free good enough for grading?

Yes. The free version of Resolve includes the scopes, wheels, curves, qualifiers, power windows and tracking covered here, and its color tools are an industry standard. Premiere's Lumetri panel does the same jobs under different names, so the method carries over.
