Add Drone Shots to Your Videos

Your aerial clips are good. The light was nice and the moves were smooth, yet the finished video feels like a slideshow of pretty shots that never adds up to anything. If you are wondering how to add drone footage to your videos so it holds attention, the answer is almost never in the footage. It is in the structure around it.

Aerials do not carry a video on their own. They are B-roll in service of a ground-level story, and they have one main job: the establishing shot that tells viewers where they are and how big the place is. This page covers what aerials are for, which handful of shots a story needs, how to cut between air and ground, and how long to hold each one. The ideas work in Premiere, Resolve, Final Cut, CapCut, or a phone app.

Who it's for

Creators with great aerial clips but flat-feeling videos. Your drone footage is genuinely good. The light was nice, the moves were smooth, the coastline looked cinematic on the little screen. So why does the finished video feel like a slideshow of pretty shots that never quite adds up to anything? The answer is almost never the footage. It is what happens between the shots — and that is a skill you can learn in an afternoon.

Where should I use drone shots in a video?

Think in terms of A-roll and B-roll. A-roll is your spine: the person, the action, the thing the video is about. Almost all drone footage is B-roll that supports it. Its strongest job is establishing: a wide aerial answers where we are, what this place is, and how big it is in the first seconds of a scene. That is why your best wide shot belongs near the start of a section, and why an aerial dropped into an intimate ground scene feels like the video lost its train of thought.

Aerials also signal a change of place or time, reveal something the ground view hid, give the eye a wide breath between tight shots, and bookend a piece with arrival or departure. They do poorly at carrying emotion, showing fine detail, or sustaining interest alone. Before keeping any aerial, ask what it tells viewers that the previous shot did not. If the only answer is that it looks cool, you have found a cut.

How do I cut between drone footage and ground footage?

Use a rhythm of wide to close and back. Open on a wide aerial to establish, land on a ground shot of the actual subject, stay on the ground for several shots where the story lives, then return to the air when you need to change location, compress time, or let the viewer breathe. Avoid stacking three or more aerials in a row, because the video floats away from the human thread.

The cleanest cuts are linked by something the eye can follow: the same subject, the same direction of motion, a specific place, or matching light. An aerial following a car down a coast road cuts naturally to a shot inside that car. A left-to-right drone move cuts well into a subject also moving left to right. Cut during motion rather than on a static hold, since the brain is busy tracking movement. Pick one or two transition styles and repeat them consistently.

How long should drone shots stay on screen?

Aerials read slowly, so duration matters. As starting points, hold an establisher for 4 to 8 seconds, a reveal for 4 to 7, a follow shot for 3 to 6, a detail push for 2 to 4, and a top-down accent for 1.5 to 3. Wider and more complex shots need longer to be understood, while tight or fast-moving ones can be quick. When trimming, cut from the front and back rather than the middle, since the strongest part of a move is usually the center.

Let music shape the structure. Mark the quiet intro, the build, the drop, and the resolution before placing shots, and land your biggest aerial on the first big swell. Cut faster during builds and use long wide aerials during the calm. Save one pull-back or rise for the final beat. When you shoot, record each clip three to five seconds longer than you think you need on both ends, so you have handles.

Common questions

How many drone shots should a video have?

Fewer than most creators use. A handful of aerials, each with a clear job such as establishing, revealing, following, or transitioning, usually beats a long chain of flyovers. One top-down shot per video is often enough. Keep the ground footage as the story and use aerials as punctuation.

What drone shots do I need for a ground-based story?

Six types cover most needs: an establisher, a follow shot, a reveal, a detail push, an orbit, and a top-down. Capture at least one wide establisher held 8 to 10 seconds, plus clips moving left to right and right to left for cutting flexibility.

Why does my drone footage feel disconnected from the rest of my video?

Usually the aerial has no link to the ground shots around it. Connect the cut through a shared subject, direction of motion, location, or light. If the only reason for a shot is that it looks impressive, it is padding, and cutting it often improves the video.

Which editing software should I use to mix drone and ground footage?

Any of them. The concepts are the same in Premiere, Resolve, Final Cut, CapCut, and phone editors, because they are about structure, timing, and motion, not buttons. Choose whichever editor you already know and focus on how each aerial shot earns its place.
