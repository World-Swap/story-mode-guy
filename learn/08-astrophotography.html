If you want to learn how to photograph the Milky Way, the obstacle is rarely your camera. Almost any DSLR or mirrorless body from the last fifteen years can do it. What goes wrong is planning, focusing in the dark, and not knowing which settings to dial in when you cannot see your screen.

A wide, fast lens, a solid tripod, and a dark sky get you most of the way. The rest is a repeatable routine: pick the right night, lock focus on a star, set a shutter time the Earth's rotation allows, and give the sky something to stand over. This is how that routine works for a first successful night out.

Who it's for

Landscape and travel shooters chasing the night sky. The band of light arcing across a truly dark sky is the Milky Way — our own galaxy, seen edge-on from the inside. Here is the part nobody tells beginners: the camera you already own can record it in gorgeous detail, and it can do it on your very first serious attempt. You do not need a telescope, a tracking mount, or a five-figure lens. You need a dark night, a few settings, and this guide.

When and where can I photograph the Milky Way?

Four things need to line up: the galactic core is up, the moon is down, the sky is dark, and the clouds cooperate. In the northern hemisphere the core is visible roughly February to October, with the best views in summer. In deep winter it sits below the horizon during darkness, though you can still shoot star fields and constellations.

Plan around the new moon. Even a half moon washes out faint structure, but you only need a moonless window, not a moonless night. Use the Bortle scale to judge darkness: aim for Bortle 4 or darker, because Bortle 6 to 9 skies lose the core to glow. A light-pollution map finds the nearest dark zone, which for many people is a one to two hour drive. Check an astronomy cloud forecast, use PhotoPills or Stellarium to see where the core rises, and scout your foreground in daylight.

How do I focus on stars at night?

Autofocus has nothing to lock onto in a dim star field, so switch the lens and camera to manual focus. Open the aperture fully and temporarily raise ISO to something like 12800 so live view is brighter. Point at the brightest star or planet, magnify the live-view display to 100 percent, and turn the focus ring slowly until the star shrinks to the smallest pinpoint. Go past the sweet spot in both directions to confirm.

Do not trust the infinity mark on the barrel, since most lenses focus past true infinity. Once sharp, put a strip of gaffer tape across the ring so it cannot drift, then drop ISO back to your shooting value. If the sky is hazy, focus on a distant light hundreds of yards away. Take a test frame and zoom to 100 percent, and recheck after any zoom change, because shifting focal length can move focus.

What are the best camera settings for the Milky Way?

Start with the widest aperture you have, such as f/2.8, ISO 3200 to 6400, manual white balance around 3900 to 4200K, and RAW. For shutter, use the 500 rule: 500 divided by focal length gives the longest exposure before stars trail. A 20mm lens on full frame gives about 25 seconds. On a crop sensor, or a high-resolution body, use 300 instead, which gives roughly 15 seconds at 20mm full frame.

If corners show smeared, comet-shaped stars, close the aperture a third to two thirds of a stop. Fire with the 2-second timer or a remote so the shutter press does not shake the camera. Read the histogram instead of the rear screen, which looks misleadingly bright in the dark. Push the data comfortably off the left edge. If a single frame is noisy, shoot 8 to 15 identical frames and stack them in free software like Sequator or Starry Landscape Stacker.

Common questions

What lens is best for photographing the Milky Way?

A fast, wide lens: f/2.8 or faster and roughly 14 to 24mm on full frame. A 14mm f/2.8 or 20mm f/1.8 is near-perfect. A kit zoom at f/3.5 to 5.6 still works, but you will have to push ISO higher and accept more noise.

What is the 500 rule in astrophotography?

It is a guideline for the longest shutter before stars visibly trail: 500 divided by your focal length in seconds. At 20mm that is about 25 seconds. On crop sensors or high-resolution cameras, use 300 instead for tighter stars, and shorten the shutter while raising ISO if in doubt.

Can I photograph the Milky Way with a phone or without a tripod?

You need a stable support, because exposures run 15 to 25 seconds and any movement ruins them. The guide covers interchangeable-lens cameras with manual controls. A sturdy tripod is essential, and a cheap wobbly one will quietly soften every frame.

Do I need a star tracker to start?

No. A tracker is a next step for longer exposures and deep-sky targets. For wide nightscapes with a fast lens, a tripod is enough. Master focusing, the 500 rule, and stacking first, since a tracker adds polar alignment and foreground-blending complexity.
