Lighting for Video

Most people who search for how to light a video are really asking why their footage looks cheap when the camera is fine. The answer is almost never the camera. Your eyes quietly even out shadows and color casts, but the sensor records exactly what hits it, so a dim room with one overhead bulb looks flat, orange, and amateur on playback.

Good video lighting comes down to three decisions: how soft the light is, which direction it comes from, and what color it is. You can make all three work with a single lamp, a bedsheet, and a window. Below are the specific moves that matter most for interviews, talking-head clips, and indoor video, starting with the one that changes your footage fastest.

Who it's for

Anyone filming interviews, talking-head, or indoor video. You can buy a better camera, a sharper lens, a wireless mic — and none of it will make your video look expensive. Lighting will. The single biggest difference between footage that looks amateur and footage that looks paid-for is not the camera; it is where the light comes from, how soft it is, and what color it is. This guide takes you from one lamp to a full setup, and it works with whatever lights you already own.

How do I make video light softer?

Softness depends on the size of the light source relative to your subject, not on how bright it is. A bare LED or direct sun is a small source and throws hard, sharp-edged shadows that exaggerate skin texture. The same light pushed through a large diffuser becomes a large source with a gentle, feathered shadow edge. You have two dials: make the source physically bigger, or move it closer. A softbox across the room acts small again; bring it in until it is just outside the frame.

Try the bedsheet test. Hang a white sheet or shower curtain about a foot in front of a work lamp or a window with direct sun and watch the shadow on a face go from harsh to flattering. Expect to lose a stop or two of brightness to diffusion. Make it up by moving the light closer, raising ISO slightly, or opening the aperture. Soft and dimmer beats hard and bright on faces.

Where should I put the key light for an interview?

Not next to the camera. Light from the lens position fills every shadow, and shadows are how viewers read depth, so frontal light makes a face look like a pancake. Start with the 45/45 position: about 45 degrees to one side of the subject and about 45 degrees above eye line, angled down, just outside the frame. Adjust until the nose shadow is a small soft triangle on the cheek that stops near the corner of the mouth, not a long streak down to the lip.

Put the key on the short side, meaning the side of the face turned away from the camera. It slims and sculpts. Then control the dark side without a second light: a white foam board gives bounce fill, a black one gives negative fill for more contrast. For a standard interview, aim for roughly a 2:1 key-to-fill ratio and keep the subject five or six feet off the background.

Why does my indoor video look orange on one side and blue on the other?

You are mixing color temperatures. Household tungsten bulbs sit around 2700 to 3200K and read orange, while daylight and most daylight LEDs sit near 5600K and read blue by comparison. The camera can white-balance for only one, so a warm lamp on one side of a face and a window on the other splits the skin into two colors that are very hard to fix in editing.

Pick one color world. Turn off the room lamps when you use a window, or gel the odd light out: CTO warms a daylight source to match tungsten, CTB cools tungsten to match daylight. Then set white balance manually to a fixed Kelvin value or a gray card reading. Auto white balance re-guesses between shots and will make your clips drift. Also set shutter to 1/60 in 60Hz countries or 1/50 in 50Hz countries to reduce LED flicker.

Common questions

Can I light a talking-head video with just one light?

Yes. A single soft key at roughly 45 degrees to the side and above eye level, plus a white foam board on the shadow side, looks better than most home-office setups. Sit your subject three to four feet off the wall so the background falls darker. Add a fill or back light only after this works.

What is the 2:1 lighting ratio?

It means the lit side of the face is twice as bright as the shadow side. It gives gentle, natural shadow and suits friendly interviews and YouTube. 4:1 adds clearer definition for documentary portraits, and 8:1 or more gets moody and dramatic. Set it by eye: start with fill off, then bring it up until shadows show detail but still read as shadow.

Is window light good enough for video?

A window out of direct sun is a large soft source and one of the best you can use. Seat the subject three to five feet from the glass at about 45 degrees and add a white board on the shadow side. The catch is that sun and clouds shift, so exposure and color drift over long takes. Diffuse the window heavily or shoot quickly.

Why do my videos show flickering bands from LED lights?

Cheap LEDs pulse faster than your eye can see, and your shutter catches it when it does not align with the pulse. Match shutter speed to local mains power: 1/60 or 1/120 at 60Hz, 1/50 or 1/100 at 50Hz. If you plan to shoot slow motion at high frame rates, record a test first, because flicker that is invisible at 24fps can strobe at 120fps.
