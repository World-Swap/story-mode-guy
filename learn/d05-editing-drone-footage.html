Editing & Grading Drone Footage

Stabilize, Color, and Make Aerials Match Your Edit

You flew a clean reveal over the coast with a dead-level horizon, then dropped it in the timeline and it looks like it belongs to a different film. It is too flat, too green, a little wobbly, and somehow both too smooth and too jittery next to your ground footage. Editing drone footage is mostly about fixing that mismatch, and the mismatch has a simple cause: drone clips arrive deliberately unfinished.

Flat profiles record a dull image on purpose so you can grade it, rolling shutter smears fast vibration into jello, and even a good gimbal leaves some drift. The fix is a fixed order of work: organize, repair motion, set timing, correct to neutral, grade the look, then match air to ground. The ideas below apply in DaVinci Resolve, Premiere Pro, and CapCut, though tool names vary.

Who it's for

Pilots whose aerial clips won't match the rest of the video. You flew the shot of your life — a clean reveal over the coast, the horizon dead level — and the moment you drop it into your timeline it looks like it came from a different film. How do I fix jello and stabilize drone footage?

Treat these as two separate problems. Jello is a warp: straight lines wobble like gelatin because a rolling-shutter sensor reads top to bottom while the drone vibrates. Instability is drift or jitter across the whole frame. Fix jello first with a rolling-shutter repair tool, such as Premiere's Rolling Shutter Repair effect or the rolling-shutter control in Resolve's stabilization panel, raising the correction only until lines look straight. Stabilizing a warped frame pushes the warp through your shot and makes it worse.

Then stabilize, gently. Warp Stabilizer and Resolve's stabilizer track points and crop in to hide the correction, so every bit of smoothing costs resolution. A gimbal is already stable, so you are polishing, not rescuing: keep smoothness low, roughly 8 to 15 percent, and use stabilize-only crop. It struggles with shots that have no static reference, like clouds or flat water. Next time, balanced clean props prevent most jello at the source.

How do I color correct and grade D-Log drone footage?

Correct first, then grade. Correction means accurate exposure, neutral white balance, and sane contrast. Use scopes, not your eyes: set exposure on the Waveform without clipping highlights like clouds and sun on water, and neutralize white balance on the Parade by finding something that should be gray, like concrete or a roof. Drone footage often lands cool, green, and hazy, so a gentle contrast boost and a touch of dehaze helps.

Flat footage must be converted to a normal image before you style it. Either use a color-managed pipeline, telling the editor the clip is DJI D-Log, or apply the manufacturer's technical LUT to Rec.709. That is a correction, not a look. A LUT alone can crush shadows on a clip that is a stop off, so fix exposure first. Then add a creative look at partial strength, around 40 to 70 percent, and grade sky and land separately with a mask or qualifier.

How do I make drone footage match my ground footage?

Matching is the deliberate step that solves the clash. Pick the hero ground shot that sets the mood for the whole piece and pin it as your reference. Correct every clip to neutral on its own first, since two clips from the same flight can differ if the sun moved. Then bring the aerials toward the reference using scopes: align overall brightness, white balance, and contrast, and compare against a still of the reference in your viewer.

Match motion too. Perfectly smooth aerials can look fake beside handheld ground clips that breathe a little, so aim for a similar amount of life in both. Match frame rate and timeline settings early, and use speed ramps with eased keyframes rather than steps, since a linear ramp reads as a glitch. Slow only as far as your frames allow: 60p footage in a 24p timeline plays cleanly at about 40 percent.

Common questions

What should I do first when editing drone footage?

Copy the original files off the SD card into a dated folder and keep the card untouched until the project is exported. Note each clip's color profile, cull junk like takeoffs and landings, and trim to the stable middle of each clip. Work from the original file, not a re-compressed export.

Why does my drone footage look green and flat?

Flat or D-Log profiles record low contrast on purpose, and drone footage often skews cool and green from flat-profile color and atmospheric haze. Convert the log footage to a standard space using color management or a technical LUT, then correct exposure and white balance using scopes.

Can I slow down drone footage smoothly?

Yes, if you shot enough frames. 4K 60p footage in a 24p timeline plays cleanly down to about 40 percent, and in 30p down to 50 percent. Below that the editor invents frames. Optical flow can help, but check detailed areas like tree canopies and waves at 100 percent for artifacts.

Do I need DaVinci Resolve to grade drone footage?

No. The workflow works in Resolve, Premiere Pro, and CapCut. Resolve and Premiere include scopes for precise correction, while CapCut does not, so there you correct by eye against a known-good reference frame. The order of work stays the same in every app.
