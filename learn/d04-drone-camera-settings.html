Drone Camera Settings for Video

Frame Rate, ND Filters & the Settings for Smooth Aerials

The drone flies beautifully, yet the footage stutters in pans, looks crunchy and over-sharpened, or comes off the card gray and lifeless. The aircraft is almost never the problem. Drone camera settings for video ship configured for snapshots, and every fix is something you can set on the ground before takeoff.

The core of smooth aerial video is one chain: pick a frame rate, lock your shutter speed to twice that number, and use an ND filter to hold that slow shutter in bright light. Everything else, from resolution and bitrate to picture profile, is refinement on top of that. This page walks through the shutter rule, how to choose an ND filter by sky, and the quality settings worth changing. Menu names differ between brands, so look for the function rather than an exact path.

Who it's for

Pilots whose footage looks juddery, over-sharp, or flat. Your drone flies beautifully. It holds position in wind, glides on command, and shows you the world from a place your feet will never stand. So why does the footage look like a bad slideshow — stuttery pans, plasticky over-sharp edges, or washed-out gray skies? The problem is almost never the aircraft. It is the camera settings, and every one of them is something you can fix on the ground before you take off.

What shutter speed should I use for drone video?

Use the 180-degree shutter rule: set shutter speed to roughly twice your frame rate. That means 1/50s at 24 or 25fps, 1/60s at 30fps, 1/120s at 60fps, and 1/240s at 120fps. Most cameras have no exact 1/48s, so 1/50s is the accepted stand-in. This gives each frame the right amount of motion blur, enough to blend frames into smooth motion without smearing.

Left on Auto, the camera raises the shutter far past 1/1000s in bright light to protect exposure, and that is the number-one cause of juddery aerials. Each frame freezes hard, and pans strobe. Switch to manual or shutter priority and lock the speed yourself. To see the difference on the ground, shoot five seconds of passing cars at 1/1000s and again at 1/50s. The fast clip stutters and the slow one glides. Too slow causes its own problem, with smeared motion and visible vibration, which usually happens at dusk if you forget to remove the ND filter.

Which ND filter do I need for drone video?

A slow shutter in bright sun lets in far too much light, so an ND filter acts as sunglasses for the lens. Each step up in number blocks one more stop: ND4 is 2 stops, ND8 is 3, ND16 is 4, ND32 is 5, and ND64 is 6. As starting points, use ND4 to 8 for overcast, ND8 to 16 for cloudy-bright, ND16 for hazy sun, ND32 for full sun, and ND64 for midday glare, snow, water, or beach.

To choose in the field, set your frame rate, lock the shutter to twice it, set ISO to base (usually 100), then look at the exposure. If it is too bright, add a stronger ND until it sits correctly with the shutter still locked. Re-check every time the light changes. An ND64 left on into golden hour pushes your ISO up and adds noise, while an ND4 at noon puts you back in fast-shutter judder. Buy a matched set of ND8, 16, 32, and 64, and favor color-neutral filters.

What resolution, bitrate, and picture profile should I use?

Shoot 4K when you can. It gives you a sharp master and room to crop, reframe, or stabilize while still delivering a clean 1080p final. Use the highest bitrate available, and H.265 (HEVC) if your editing computer can handle it, since it holds more quality per megabit and keeps skies cleaner. H.264 is more compatible and easier on older machines. Use a fast microSD card rated for sustained video, such as V30 or higher, and reformat it in the drone before a shoot.

Picture profile is a real tradeoff. A normal profile looks finished straight from the card but bakes in contrast and color permanently, leaving less room to recover highlights. A flat or log profile like D-Log keeps maximum latitude but looks gray until graded. Pick normal if you need to deliver fast or are still learning, and flat if you will color grade every clip.

Common questions

Why does my drone video look choppy when I pan?

Almost always the shutter speed is too fast, usually because the camera is on Auto in bright light with no ND filter. Each frame freezes sharp, so motion strobes. Lock the shutter to twice your frame rate, such as 1/60s at 30fps, and add an ND filter to control exposure.

What frame rate is best for drone footage?

It depends on the look. 24fps gives a filmic feel, 30fps is a smooth standard for real estate and social, and 60fps or 120fps are for shots you plan to slow down. Decide your project's base rate first, then shoot slow-motion shots at a higher rate.

Do I really need ND filters on a drone?

If you want smooth motion in daylight, yes. The correct video shutter is slow, and in bright sun that slow shutter would overexpose without a filter. Without ND you are forced into a fast shutter, which is what makes aerials judder. A matched set covers a full day of changing light.

Should I shoot D-Log or the normal profile?

Shoot normal if you want to deliver quickly or do not plan to grade, since it looks good with no work. Choose a flat profile like D-Log only if you will color grade, because it looks washed out straight off the card but gives you more latitude to adjust exposure and color.
