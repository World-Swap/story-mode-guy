What should a photography cheat sheet include?

The core is the exposure triangle: aperture controls depth of field, shutter controls motion blur, and ISO controls noise. A stop is a doubling or halving of light, so opening the aperture one stop lets you use a shutter twice as fast or an ISO half as high. Decide what matters in the scene first. If motion matters, set shutter first; if background blur does, set aperture first; let Auto ISO handle the rest.

Then add quick lookups. How do I use camera settings cards in the field?

Treat every number as a starting point, not a law. Pick the scene on the recipe card, dial in the settings, take a test shot, check the brightness, and nudge. Build the habit of a three-second glance before each shot: is the shutter fast enough to be sharp, is the aperture giving enough in focus, and is ISO as low as the light allows?

For scenes not on the card, ask three questions in order. Is anything moving, so set the shutter to freeze it? How much needs to be in focus, so set the aperture? Is it bright enough, so raise ISO last? Print the cards, laminate them to survive rain and the bottom of a bag, and keep the PDF on your phone. After a few outings the numbers live in your hands and you will need the card less.

Why are my photos blurry, dark, grainy, or the wrong color?

Name the symptom first, then change one thing at a time. If the photo is too dark, slow the shutter, open the aperture, or raise ISO, in that order for stills. Too bright is the reverse. A blurry whole frame is camera shake, so raise the shutter to at least 1 divided by focal length, add support, or turn stabilization on. A blurry moving subject needs a faster shutter, continuous AF, and burst mode.

If the wrong thing is sharp, focus missed, so use single-point AF on the eye or turn on Eye-AF. Grain means ISO is too high, so add light or open the aperture. Wrong colors mean white balance is off, so match the preset to the light or shoot RAW and fix it later. If a fix would push the shutter below your handholding limit, raise ISO instead, since sharp and grainy beats clean and blurry.

Common questions

What are good starting camera settings for portraits?

Around f/2.8, 1/200s, and ISO 100 to 400, with single-point or Eye AF on the near eye. Go to f/4 for two people so both stay sharp. These are starting points, so check the shot and adjust to your light.

What shutter speed should I use to freeze motion?

As a guide: 1/125s for a still portrait, 1/250s for walking, 1/500s for kids and pets, 1/1000s for running or sport, and 1/2000s for birds and cars. Motion across the frame needs a faster shutter than motion coming toward you.

What is the sharpest aperture on most lenses?

Most lenses are sharpest around f/5.6 to f/8. Avoid f/22, because diffraction softens the whole image. For more depth of field without stopping down too far, step back from the subject or focus about a third of the way into the scene.

Should I use auto white balance or a preset?

Auto works for most scenes. If you shoot RAW, leave it on Auto and correct later with no quality loss. For JPEG, pick the preset that matches the light, such as Tungsten under warm bulbs or Cloudy for gentle warmth. Mixed light is the hard case.
