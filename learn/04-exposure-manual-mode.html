Learning manual mode feels hard because three separate controls all change brightness, and each also changes something else in the picture. Auto handles the brightness, but it also decides whether your background is blurry, whether motion is frozen, and whether the file is grainy. Those are the choices that separate a snapshot from a photograph.

If you want to get off auto on your DSLR or mirrorless camera, the exposure triangle is the place to start. Here is what aperture, shutter speed, and ISO each actually do, why a smaller f-number means a bigger opening, and how to trade one against another to keep your exposure steady.

Who it's for

New DSLR & mirrorless owners intimidated by the mode dial. That green AUTO mode is the training wheels you were supposed to take off. This weekend, you finally do. By Sunday night you will understand exactly what your camera is doing when it makes a picture — and you will be the one telling it what to do, on purpose, every single time.

What the exposure triangle actually means

Exposure is how much light reaches the sensor. Aperture is the size of the opening in the lens, shutter speed is how long it stays open, and ISO is how much the signal is amplified afterward. Each one also has a side effect: aperture changes depth of field, shutter speed changes how motion looks, and ISO changes how noisy the image is. Whenever you change one for brightness, you get its side effect too.

Light is measured in stops, and one stop is a doubling or halving. Open the aperture one stop, slow the shutter one stop, or raise ISO one stop, and the image gets twice as bright. Because they share this unit, you can trade between them. Speed the shutter up a stop to freeze motion, open the aperture a stop to pay the light back, and the overall exposure stays level. That trade is the core skill of manual shooting.

Why a small f-number means a bigger opening, and how that shapes the background

An f-number is a fraction: focal length divided by the diameter of the opening. So f/2 is a bigger hole than f/16, just as one half is bigger than one sixteenth. Small number, big opening, more light, blurrier background. Big number, small opening, less light, more in focus.

For portraits, food, and single objects, shoot wide, around f/1.8 to f/4, and nail focus on the eye because the sharp zone can be thinner than a face. Focus on the near eye at f/1.8 and the far eye may go soft, so stop down to f/2.8 to f/4 for breathing room. For landscapes and group shots, use f/8 to f/16. Most lenses are sharpest two or three stops from wide open, typically f/5.6 to f/8, and past f/16 diffraction softens the whole frame, so do not go further than you need.

A simple way to move from auto to manual without frustration

You do not have to jump straight to full manual. Start in Aperture Priority (A or Av) when depth of field is your priority, or Shutter Priority (S or Tv) when motion is. You choose the setting that matters creatively, and the camera picks the rest. Many photographers stay in these modes for months, and that is a perfectly respectable place to work.

When you are ready for full manual, learn to read the built-in light meter, which shows whether the camera thinks you are over or under exposed. Pick the setting that matters for your subject first, then adjust the other two until the meter sits near the middle, and check the result on the screen. A reasonable portrait starting point outdoors in good light is f/2.8, 1/250s, ISO 200. Treat starting points as a place to begin, then adjust from what you see.

Common questions

What is the exposure triangle in simple terms?

It is the relationship between aperture, shutter speed, and ISO. All three control how bright the photo is, and each also changes one other thing: depth of field, motion blur, or noise. Changing one means you may need to adjust another to keep the brightness the same.

What does a stop of light mean?

A stop is a doubling or halving of light. Opening the aperture one stop, slowing the shutter one stop, or raising ISO one stop each makes the image twice as bright. Because the units match, you can trade stops between controls and keep the exposure the same.

Is aperture priority or manual mode better for beginners?

Aperture Priority is a good starting point because you control the background blur and the camera chooses the shutter speed. Shutter Priority works the same way for motion. Move to full manual once you can read the meter and know what each change will do.

Why are my wide-aperture photos not sharp?

At f/1.8 the in-focus zone can be thinner than a face, so a small focus error shows up as softness. Lenses also tend to be less sharp wide open. Try f/2.8 to f/4 and focus carefully on the near eye. For maximum sharpness, f/5.6 to f/8 is usually best.
