Most people buy a drone and shoot video, then scroll back through the clips and realize the best moments were single frames. Drone photography is the practice of flying to make those frames on purpose: sharp, high-resolution stills that print large and hold detail a video screenshot never will.

The view from above is a different visual language. The horizon can vanish, foreground and background collapse into one plane, and the landscape turns into lines, shapes, patterns, and texture. Below are the core habits for better aerial photos: how to compose a top-down shot, how to stitch a panorama that actually lines up, and how to expose for the harsh light you get in the sky.

Who it's for

Pilots who want striking stills, not just video. Most people buy a drone and shoot video. Then they scroll back through hundreds of clips and realize the best moments were single frames — a road cutting a green field in half, a coastline drawn in foam, a city grid glowing at dusk. This guide is about deliberately making those frames. Not grabbing lucky screenshots from footage, but flying to make photographs: sharp, high-resolution, print-worthy stills that only exist because you were two hundred feet in the air.

How do you compose a top-down drone photo?

The straight-down shot, with the gimbal pitched to -90 degrees, removes the horizon and turns the ground into a flat graphic surface, so your usual instincts stop working. Start by finding one strong element: a single road, a lone boat, a solitary tree. Turn on the rule-of-thirds grid and line up dominant edges so roads run true, because crooked geometry reads as a mistake from up there.

Then decide on purpose. Either fill the frame edge to edge with pattern, or let a small subject float in negative space. The weak middle, where a subject neither fills nor floats, is what ruins most top-down shots. Look for repetition plus one interruption, like a single red car in a lot of gray ones. Since everything is at roughly the same focus distance, depth of field is a non-issue: a starting point of f/4 to f/5.6, 1/500s or faster, ISO 100, RAW. Scan the corners for your own drone's shadow before you press the shutter.

How do you shoot a drone panorama that stitches cleanly?

Stitching software needs two things: overlap and consistency. Overlap every frame by 30 to 50 percent so the software has matching detail to blend in the seams. Then lock everything. Use manual exposure so shutter, aperture, and ISO stay the same across the sweep, set focus to infinity, and fix white balance to a preset like Daylight. Auto white balance re-reads the sky each frame and leaves you cyan on one edge and warm on the other.

There are three useful types: a wide pano (one row of frames, great for coastlines and skylines), a grid pano (multiple rows for huge resolution), and a sphere (a full 360 you can flatten into a tiny planet). If you fly one by hand, hover in one spot and only yaw between frames, which keeps parallax low. The onboard auto-stitch tends to flatten the file, so for print-size results keep the individual RAW frames and stitch them in your editor.

How do you expose aerial photos in harsh light?

Shoot RAW, not JPEG. A JPEG throws away most of the sensor data, and aerial scenes with a bright sky over dark land need all of it. Do not trust the controller screen, which looks too dark in daylight and tempts you to overexpose. Read the histogram and watch the right edge: if it slams against the wall, the highlights are clipped and a blown sky is gone for good. Turn on the highlight warning and protect the highlights first, since shadows lift cleanly in editing.

When the scene truly exceeds one frame, use auto exposure bracketing: 3 shots for normal contrast, 5 for a brutal sunrise, spaced about 0.7 to 1 stop apart, merged later. Bracket only in calm air, because drift between frames causes ghosting on hard edges. Skip it on flat overcast light or most top-downs. A single RAW from a decent sensor often holds enough range that you never need to bracket.

Common questions

What camera settings are best for drone photography?

A solid starting point is RAW format, ISO 100, aperture around f/4 to f/5.6, and a shutter of 1/500s or faster for straight-down shots. Use Daylight white balance instead of Auto, and judge exposure with the histogram rather than the screen. Adjust from there for wind, light, and your sensor size.

When is the best time of day for aerial photos?

The hour after sunrise and before sunset. A low sun throws long shadows that reveal texture and shape from above, while midday light flattens the land. Blue hour, the 20 to 30 minutes around sunrise and sunset, suits cities with glowing windows, but you need steady air for the longer exposures.

Do I need HDR bracketing for every drone photo?

No. A single RAW file from a decent sensor can hold 11 to 14 stops of range, often enough to recover highlights and lift shadows. Bracket only when the histogram shows a scene, like a backlit sunrise, exceeds one frame, and only when the air is calm enough for the drone to hold still.

Do I need a license to sell my drone photos?

Often yes. In the US, paid drone work generally falls under FAA Part 107 rather than the recreational rules. This is general information, not legal advice. Verify current requirements with the FAA, or your national aviation authority, and check airspace and altitude limits before every flight.
