Shoot Better Video on Your Phone

Phone video looks like phone video because of habits, not hardware. The sensor in your pocket is good enough for festival films and ad campaigns. What gives it away is a picture that keeps refocusing, a shaky frame, a face lost in a dim room, and a voice that sounds like it was recorded in a bathroom.

If you are searching for how to shoot better video on your phone, start with the cheap, free fixes in this order: a few settings you change once, locking focus and exposure, using window light, and getting a microphone near the mouth. None of it needs a new phone, and all of it works on iPhone and Android.

Who it's for

Creators and small businesses filming on a smartphone. The phone in your hand shoots sharper, more color-accurate footage than the film cameras that made blockbusters a generation ago. So why does your video still look like "phone video" and someone else's looks like a commercial? It is almost never the phone. It is a short list of habits — settings, light, sound, and how you hold the thing — and every one of them is free to fix.

What are the best video settings for iPhone and Android?

Set them once and forget them. Shoot 4K, pick 24 or 30fps, and use the High Efficiency (HEVC) format. 4K matters even if the video ends up on Instagram, because the extra pixels let you crop, straighten a horizon or punch in for a tighter shot without going soft. A wide 4K interview gives you a free close-up in a 1080p edit.

Use 24fps for story and mood, 30fps for tutorials and talking-head work, and 60fps only for clips you will slow down. Shooting everything at 60 makes video look like live TV and doubles file sizes. Commit to one frame rate per project, since mixing 24 and 30 makes motion stutter. ProRes and log modes exist, but they produce huge files and flat footage that needs grading, so most creators are better off staying on HEVC.

How do I stop my phone video from changing focus and brightness?

That pulsing is the biggest amateur giveaway on a phone, and the fix is free. Frame your shot, then tap and hold on your subject until the lock appears. On iPhone it says AE/AF Lock, on Android you usually see a lock icon. Focus and exposure are now frozen and will not drift when someone walks through the frame or a cloud passes.

After locking, slide the exposure control up or down to taste. When in doubt go slightly darker: a face that is a touch dim can be lifted in the edit, but a blown window is gone for good. Remember that locked focus means locked distance, so if you step closer or farther the subject softens. Re-lock at the new position, or use a pro app such as Blackmagic Camera if you need to pull focus smoothly.

How can I get steady, well-lit phone footage without a gimbal?

Your body is a tripod you already own. Hold the phone with both hands, tuck your elbows into your ribs, exhale as you start and lean on a wall or doorframe when you can. For a pan, plant your feet, lock the phone to your chest and rotate from the hips rather than twisting your wrists. Leave standard stabilization on, but switch heavy modes like Action or Super Steady off when you are on a tripod, since they crop in and can add a floaty wobble.

Then fix the light, because phones hate dim rooms. Face your subject toward a big window, never with the window behind them, and soften harsh sun with a sheer curtain. Turn off overhead lights, which carve shadows into eye sockets. A $15 to $40 LED panel or ring light placed in front and slightly above eye level handles everything else.

Common questions

Is it worth using a pro camera app instead of the stock camera?

Sometimes. The native app is excellent for talking-head, social and product video once you lock focus and exposure. Reach for an app like Blackmagic Camera or Filmic Pro when you need manual shutter and white balance, focus pulls, zebras and peaking, or log recording. Otherwise the stock app wins on speed and reliability.

What should I buy first to improve phone video?

Buy in this order: a tripod or phone grip, then an external microphone, then a small LED light, and only later a gimbal. A tripod removes shake from every static shot, and a lav or wireless mic fixes your weakest link. Clip-on phone lenses mostly add flare and softness, so skip those.

Should I film vertical or horizontal?

Frame for where the video will live. Use 9:16 for Reels, TikTok, Shorts and Stories, and 16:9 for YouTube and websites. If you are unsure, shoot horizontal in 4K. The resolution leaves enough pixels to crop a clean vertical version in editing, so one shoot can feed both.

Why does my phone video sound so bad?

The built-in mic is tiny, omnidirectional and usually far from the speaker, so it captures room echo and background noise as much as the voice. Distance is the enemy. Get a clip-on or wireless lav a few inches from the mouth, record in a soft room, and play back ten seconds before the real take.
