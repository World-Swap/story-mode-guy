💡The one habit that ties it all together

Before every shot, glance at three things: shutter (is it fast enough to be sharp?), aperture (is enough in focus?), and ISO (is it as low as the light allows?). These cards exist to make that three-second check automatic.

CARD 1The Exposure Triangle

Three controls decide how bright your photo is — and each one also changes how the picture looks. That second effect is the whole game. Change one, and you trade for a side effect somewhere else.

🔘 Aperture

The hole in the lens. Lower f-number = bigger hole = more light and a blurrier background. Controls depth of field.

⏱️ Shutter

How long the sensor is exposed. Faster = less light but freezes motion. Controls motion blur.

📈 ISO

Sensor sensitivity. Higher = brighter but grainier. Controls noise.

What each control does — and what it costs

ControlTypical rangeTurn it up to…Side effect
Aperturef/1.8 – f/16Blur background (low f) / keep all sharp (high f)Depth of field shrinks or grows
Shutter30s – 1/4000sFreeze motion (fast) / show motion (slow)Motion blur & hand-shake risk
ISO100 – 12800Brighten in dark scenesNoise / grain increases
📷The "stop" is the shared unit

A stop is a doubling or halving of light. Open one stop of aperture, and you can use a shutter twice as fast — or an ISO half as high — for the same brightness. That's how you trade between the three without changing exposure.

The exposure triangle in one line

Pick the look you need first — frozen or flowing, deep or dreamy — then balance the other two to keep the brightness right.

💡Which one to set first

Decide what matters most in the scene. Motion? Set shutter first (use S / Tv mode). Background blur or sharpness front-to-back? Set aperture first (use A / Av mode). Let the camera and Auto ISO handle the rest.

Nine everyday scenes, each with a starting point you can dial in fast. Set these, take a shot, check the brightness, and nudge. When light is dim, let Auto ISO climb before you slow the shutter below the numbers here.

🎓Build your own recipe in ten seconds

Ask: Is anything moving? Set the shutter to freeze it. How much do I want in focus? Set the aperture. Is it bright enough? Raise ISO last. Three questions, in that order, and you've built a recipe for any scene not on this card.

CARD 3Aperture & Depth of Field

Aperture is the f-number. It sets how much of your scene is in sharp focus — the depth of field. Small number, shallow focus, creamy background. Big number, deep focus, everything sharp.

f-numberOpeningDepth of fieldBest for
f/1.4 – f/2Very wideVery shallowDramatic portraits, low light, background blur
f/2.8 – f/4WideShallowPortraits, pets, subject separation
f/5.6 – f/8MediumModerate–deepGroups, street, general sharpness sweet spot
f/11 – f/16NarrowDeepLandscapes, architecture, front-to-back sharp
f/22+Very narrowDeepest (but softer)Rarely worth it — diffraction blurs the whole frame
🌸 Use a WIDE aperture (low f) when…
  • You want a blurred, creamy background
  • Light is low and you need every photon
  • One subject should pop from its surroundings
🏔️ Use a NARROW aperture (high f) when…
  • You need everything sharp, near to far
  • Shooting landscapes or group photos
  • Detail across the frame matters most
The sharpness sweet spot

Almost every lens is sharpest around f/5.6 to f/8. When ultimate detail matters and you don't need shallow focus, live there.

⚠️Don't chase f/22 for sharpness

Stopping down past f/11–f/16 triggers diffraction, which softens the entire image. To get more depth of field without going too narrow, step back or focus about a third of the way into the scene instead.

💡Three things change depth of field

It's not just aperture. Getting closer to your subject and using a longer focal length both shrink depth of field too. That's why a close-up portrait at 85mm f/2.8 blurs the background far more than a wide landscape shot at the same f-number.

CARD 4Shutter Speed

Shutter speed is how long the sensor sees light. Fast freezes; slow flows. It controls both motion blur in your subject and shake blur from your own hands.

Shutter speedEffectUse for
1/2000s – 1/1000sFreezes fast actionSports, birds, cars, splashing water
1/500s – 1/250sFreezes everyday motionKids, pets, walking people
1/125s – 1/60sSharp for still subjectsPortraits, street, handheld general
1/30s – 1/4sMotion starts to blurPanning, intentional blur, low light on support
1s – 30sLong streaks & smooth waterLight trails, waterfalls, stars (tripod only)

The handholding rule

To avoid shake blur, your shutter should be at least 1 divided by your focal length. At 50mm, use 1/50s or faster. At 200mm, use 1/200s or faster — long lenses magnify shake as much as they magnify the subject.

⚠️Crop-sensor correction

On a crop-sensor body, multiply focal length by the crop factor first — 1.5× for most brands, 1.6× for Canon. A 50mm lens then wants about 1/80s, not 1/50s. When unsure, round up.

Shutter speeds to freeze a subjectFIELD-TESTED
Still portrait → 1/125sWalking → 1/250sKids & pets → 1/500sRunning / sport → 1/1000sBirds & cars → 1/2000s

Motion across the frame needs a faster shutter than motion coming toward you. Sideways streaks the most.

🎓Stabilization helps shake, not subjects

In-body (IBIS) or in-lens stabilization buys 2–5 stops of slower shutter for your shake — but it does nothing for a moving subject. Turn it off on a tripod, where it can hunt and add blur.

CARD 5ISO & Noise

ISO brightens the photo when aperture and shutter can't gather enough light. The cost is noise — the grainy speckle that grows as ISO climbs. The rule: keep ISO as low as the scene allows, but never so low that the shot goes blurry.

LightingISO to startNotes
Bright sun100Base ISO — cleanest possible image
Overcast / shade200–400Still very clean
Indoors, good light800–1600Minor noise, easily usable
Dim indoors / evening3200–6400Visible grain, still a keeper
Very dark / night6400–12800+Noisy but rescues the shot
The noise tradeoff

A grainy sharp photo is a keeper. A clean blurry one is deleted. When forced to choose, raise ISO and keep the shutter fast.

📷Base ISO is your cleanest setting

Every sensor has a base ISO — usually 100 — where it produces the least noise and the most dynamic range. Use it whenever there's enough light, especially on a tripod where you don't need speed.

Auto ISO — set it and forget it

  • Turn on Auto ISO so the camera raises it only as needed.
  • Set a maximum (say 6400) so noise never runs away.
  • Set a minimum shutter speed (a "shutter floor") so it never slows below your safe handheld speed.
  • Now shoot in Aperture or Shutter Priority and let ISO float underneath.
💡Expose it right in camera

Noise gets worse when you brighten a dark photo later. Getting the exposure right — or slightly bright — at capture keeps noise lower than rescuing an underexposed file in editing.

CARD 6White Balance

White balance tells the camera what "white" looks like under the current light, so colors come out true instead of orange or blue. Auto handles most scenes, but knowing the presets lets you fix — or stylize — the mood on purpose.

PresetKelvin (approx)Use when…
Tungsten / Incandescent3200KUnder warm household bulbs — removes the orange
Fluorescent4000KOffice / store lighting — cuts the green cast
Daylight5200KDirect sun, midday — neutral baseline
Flash5500KUsing a flash — matches its color
Cloudy6000KOvercast — adds gentle warmth
Shade7000KOpen shade — counters the cool blue

The Kelvin scale, plainly

Low Kelvin = warm/orange light; high Kelvin = cool/blue light. To warm up a photo, set a higher Kelvin number than the actual light; to cool it down, set a lower one. That's the whole trick.

🔥 Want it warmer?

Pick Cloudy or Shade (or a higher Kelvin). Great for sunsets, skin tones, and cozy interiors.

❄️ Want it cooler?

Pick Tungsten (or a lower Kelvin). Useful for crisp blue-hour and clean, clinical looks.

🎓Shoot RAW and stop worrying

In RAW, white balance is fully adjustable after the fact with zero quality loss. Leave it on Auto, and correct in editing later. JPEG bakes it in — so for JPEG, pick the matching preset in the moment.

⚠️Mixed light is the hard case

Warm lamps and cool window light in one frame can't both be neutral — one will cast. Pick the light on your subject and balance for that, or embrace the contrast as part of the mood. A single dominant light source is always easiest to balance.

CARD 7Autofocus

Two decisions run your autofocus: the mode (does it lock once or track continuously?) and the area (does the camera pick the spot, or do you?). Match both to your subject and your keeper rate jumps.

Focus modes — lock vs. track

ModeNames by brandUse for
SingleAF-S / One-ShotStill subjects — locks focus once, holds it
ContinuousAF-C / AI ServoMoving subjects — refocuses as distance changes
AutoAF-A / AI FocusUnpredictable — switches on its own; continuous is the safer default

Area modes — who picks the spot

Area modeWhat it doesUse for
Single-pointYou place one small point exactlyPortraits, precise focus, off-center subjects
Zone / GroupA cluster of points covers an areaErratic but framed action
Wide / Auto-areaCamera chooses across the frameFast, unpredictable subjects; snapshots
Eye / Subject detectFinds and tracks eyes automaticallyPeople and animals — the modern default
⚠️The focus-and-recompose trap

Locking focus, then swinging the camera to reframe can shift the sharp plane off your subject — especially at wide apertures like f/1.8. For close portraits, move the focus point to the subject instead of recomposing.

Back-button focus — the pro habit

By default the shutter button focuses and fires, and those two jobs fight each other. Back-button focus moves focusing to a rear thumb button (often "AF-ON"), leaving the shutter to only take the picture. Focus once, then fire as many frames as you like without the camera re-hunting.

  • Still subject? Single AF (AF-S), single-point on the eye.
  • Moving subject? Continuous AF (AF-C) + burst mode.
  • People or pets? Turn on Eye / Animal detection.
  • Missing focus often? Switch off full-auto area and place the point yourself.
  • Ready to level up? Assign back-button focus and never look back.

CARD 8Composition

Settings make a photo sharp and bright; composition makes it worth looking at. Six rules cover the vast majority of strong images. Learn them as defaults, then break them on purpose.

▦ Rule of thirds

Place your subject on a third-line or where they cross, not dead center. Turn on the grid to see it.

↗️ Leading lines

Use roads, fences, and shadows to pull the eye toward your subject.

🖼️ Framing

Shoot through a doorway, branch, or arch to wrap the subject and add depth.

🦋 Symmetry

Center a reflection or repeating pattern for calm, deliberate balance.

⬜ Negative space

Leave empty room around the subject to make it feel isolated and strong.

🌫️ Depth

Include a near, middle, and far element so the flat frame feels three-dimensional.

🎯Try this on your next walk

Pick one rule and shoot only that for twenty minutes — say, leading lines. Hunting for a single idea trains your eye faster than trying to remember all six at once.

Compose first, settings second. A technically perfect photo of a boring frame is still a boring photo.
💡Watch the edges and the background

Before you press the shutter, scan the frame's edges for distractions and the background for poles "growing" out of heads. A half-step left or right often cleans up the whole shot.

CARD 9Troubleshooting Flowchart

Photo came out wrong? Find the symptom, apply the fix. Work top to bottom — the first matching row usually solves it.

If your photo is…Do this
Too darkSlow the shutter, open the aperture (lower f), or raise ISO — in that order for stills. Or nudge exposure compensation +.
Too brightSpeed up the shutter, close the aperture (higher f), or lower ISO. Or nudge exposure compensation −.
Blurry (whole frame)Camera shake — raise shutter to the handholding rule, add support, turn on stabilization.
Blurry (moving subject)Subject motion — use Shutter Priority, pick a freezing speed, add continuous AF + burst.
Blurry (wrong thing sharp)Missed focus — single-point AF on the eye, or turn on Eye-AF.
GrainyISO too high — add light, open the aperture, or lower ISO and slow the shutter on support.
Wrong colorsWhite balance off — match the preset to the light (or shoot RAW and fix later).

The fix, step by step

  1. Name the problem first. Zoom in on the back of the camera and decide: brightness, sharpness, grain, or color? Don't change settings blindly.
  2. Change one thing at a time. Adjust a single control, shoot again, compare. Two changes at once hide which one worked.
  3. Protect sharpness above all. If a fix would drop your shutter below the handholding rule, raise ISO instead. Sharp-and-grainy beats clean-and-blurry.
  4. Re-check after every fix. One change often shifts the exposure — glance at brightness again before you move on.

Before-you-shoot checklist

  • ISO reset to base (100) or Auto with a sensible ceiling
  • Shutter fast enough for the handholding rule and any motion
  • Aperture set for the depth of field you want
  • Focus mode matched to a still or moving subject
  • White balance on Auto or matched to the light
  • Card has space and battery is charged
  • Front element clean and lens cap in your pocket
