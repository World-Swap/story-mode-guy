Most photos that disappoint people aren't technically bad. They're badly composed. Good photography composition is the difference between "I was there" and "look at this," and after years of shooting the Santa Cruz coast, I can tell you it matters more than the camera in your hand. These are the composition tips I actually use, and every one works on a phone or a full-frame body.

Start by deciding what the photo is about

Before you raise the camera, say the subject out loud in a few words: "the surfer," "the pier at dusk," "my daughter's face." If you can't, the frame will wander. Everything else on this list is a way of pointing the viewer at that one thing and keeping their eye from leaving.

Use the rule of thirds, then know when to break it

Turn on your grid overlay. It divides the frame into nine boxes, and the rule of thirds says to put your subject near one of the four intersections instead of dead center. A lone cypress on the left third with open sky to its right feels balanced and alive. Centered, it often feels like a mugshot.

  • Horizons: place them on the top or bottom third line. Pick the one with the more interesting half, so a dramatic sunset gets two thirds of the frame and flat sand gets one.
  • Eyes: in portraits, put the nearer eye on a top-third line.
  • Break it on purpose: perfect symmetry, like a reflection in a tide pool or a tunnel of redwoods, deserves the center.

Find leading lines

Leading lines are any lines that pull the eye toward your subject: a boardwalk, a fence, a wave's edge sliding up the sand, a row of railings on the wharf. The trick is to start the line near the bottom corner of the frame so it has a long path to travel. Crouch down and move a step left or right until the line points at your subject instead of just crossing it.

Always look for a foreground

This is the single habit that most improves landscapes. A flat scene of distant cliffs looks like a postcard from a gas station. Put something interesting in the bottom third, such as wet rocks, kelp, wildflowers, or a tire track, and suddenly the picture has depth. On a phone, get within a foot or two of the foreground object and let the background fall away behind it. On a camera, use a wide lens around 16 to 24mm and stop down to f/8 or f/11 so near and far are both sharp.

Give your subject room with negative space

Empty space isn't wasted space. A small figure on a huge, quiet beach says something about scale and calm that a tight crop can't. Leave breathing room, especially in front of a moving or looking subject. If a person faces right, give them space on the right. Cropping tight against their gaze makes the image feel cramped, like they're about to walk out of the frame.

Frame within the frame

Doorways, arches, tree branches, and windows create a second border inside your image and tell the viewer where to look. I use the dark underside of the wharf all the time to frame bright water beyond it. Expose for the bright area and let the frame go dark.

A quick routine before you press the shutter

When I'm working a scene, I run a fast mental checklist. It takes about ten seconds and has saved a lot of frames.

  1. Scan the edges. Look for trash cans, stray branches, or a pole growing out of someone's head. Move a foot and it's gone.
  2. Check the horizon. A tilted ocean looks wrong because water is level. Use the grid line to fix it.
  3. Change your height. Shoot once at eye level, then once low and once high. The best angle is rarely the first one.
  4. Take a step closer. Most beginners shoot too far back. Fill the frame with what matters.
  5. Shoot a vertical and a horizontal. It's free insurance.

Practice with constraints

Pick one idea per outing. Spend a Saturday morning at Natural Bridges shooting only with foreground elements, or an evening on West Cliff using only leading lines. Constraints force you to see differently, and within a few weeks you start composing automatically.

Composition is a habit, not a talent, and it grows every time you slow down and look at the edges of your frame. If you want to see these ideas at work, browse my photo gallery and try to spot the thirds, lines, and foregrounds in each shot.
