If you want to know how to photograph waves, start with one decision: do you want to freeze the water or blur it? Everything else follows from your shutter speed. I've been shooting the coast around Santa Cruz for years, from Steamer Lane to the cliffs at Natural Bridge, and the ocean rewards both approaches. They just look nothing alike.

Freezing the Spray: Fast Shutter Speeds

Frozen waves feel powerful. You see every droplet hanging in the air and the texture on the face of a swell. For that look, I start at 1/1000 second and go faster when the surf is big or a wave is exploding against rock.

  • Shutter speed: 1/1000 to 1/2000 for crashing spray. 1/500 is fine for a calmer rolling wave.
  • Aperture: f/8 gives you enough sharpness across a scene with rocks and water at different distances.
  • ISO: Let it float. On a bright day you'll sit near ISO 100 to 400. Under overcast skies, ISO 800 is a fair trade for a sharp frame.
  • Focus: Continuous autofocus (AF-C) with a single point or small zone, aimed at where the wave will break.
  • Drive mode: High-speed burst. The perfect frame lasts a fraction of a second, so shoot sequences and cull later.

A 70-200mm lens is my go-to for this. It lets me compress the scene and isolate a single breaking section without standing anywhere dangerous.

Silky Water: Long Exposure Seascapes

Slow shutter speeds turn churning water into mist and give tide pools, receding foam and rocky coves a calm, dreamy quality. This is the classic long exposure water look, and it needs a tripod, no exceptions.

  1. Mount the camera on a sturdy tripod and set the legs low and wide. Wind and water spray will shake a light setup.
  2. Switch to manual or aperture priority, set ISO to its base value, and choose f/11 to f/16.
  3. Check the shutter speed your camera picks. In daylight it will probably be 1/15 or faster, which isn't slow enough.
  4. Add a neutral density filter. A 6-stop ND often lands you around 1 to 4 seconds in bright light. A 10-stop pushes you to 30 seconds or more.
  5. Use a 2-second timer or a remote release so pressing the button doesn't add shake.

The sweet spot for most seascapes is 0.5 to 4 seconds when waves are washing up a beach. You keep a hint of texture in the foam. Go longer than 10 seconds and the water flattens into fog, which can be beautiful on a jagged shoreline but dull on an open beach.

Best Light for Ocean Photography

Golden hour is great, but the hour after sunset is often better for silky water. The sky glows, the shadows soften, and you can use slow shutter speeds without a filter. Santa Cruz faces south, so sunsets over the water are best in winter when the sun sets farther toward the southwest.

This is where low-light technique matters. Tripod discipline, base ISO and careful exposure make the difference between a muddy dusk shot and a clean one.

Composition Tips That Work on the Coast

  • Anchor the frame. A rock, a piling or a bit of dry sand in the foreground gives the eye somewhere to start.
  • Use leading lines. Receding wash on wet sand draws a path toward the horizon in long exposures.
  • Keep the horizon level. A tilted ocean horizon looks wrong at once. Use your camera's grid or electronic level.
  • Time your shots. For long exposures, trigger the shutter as a wave starts to retreat so the whole exposure captures movement.

Stay Safe Photographing the Surf

This part matters more than any camera setting. The Pacific is unpredictable, and sneaker waves have swept photographers off rocks and beaches up and down the California coast. My rules:

  • Never turn your back on the ocean. Watch the sets for at least ten minutes before you set up.
  • Stay above the wet line on the sand or rocks. If it's wet, a wave has reached it.
  • Check tide tables and swell forecasts before you go. High tide combined with a big swell is when most accidents happen.
  • Wear shoes with grip, and don't go out onto slick rocks alone.
  • No photo is worth your life. Zoom in instead of walking closer.

Protect Your Gear

Salt spray is quietly brutal on cameras. I carry a microfiber cloth for the front element and a clear UV filter I don't mind replacing. Wipe the body down with a slightly damp cloth when you get home, and don't change lenses on the beach if you can avoid it.
