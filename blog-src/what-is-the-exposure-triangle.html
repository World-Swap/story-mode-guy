If you've ever wondered what the exposure triangle actually is, here's the short version: it's the relationship between three settings, aperture, shutter speed, and ISO, that together decide how bright your photo is. Change one and you have to adjust another to keep the same brightness. That's the whole idea. I've been shooting along the Santa Cruz coast for years, and I still think about every frame in these terms, because each dial also changes the way the picture looks, not just how bright it is.

Think of exposure as a bucket of light

Picture a bucket under a rain shower. You can fill it by using a wider bucket, by leaving it out longer, or by having a shower that's just heavier. Exposure works the same way. Aperture is how wide the bucket is, shutter speed is how long you leave it out, and ISO is how much you amplify what landed in it. A correct exposure is simply a bucket that's filled to a sensible level.

The reason it's called a triangle is that all three are linked. They aren't equal partners, though. Each one has a side effect, and that side effect is the real reason to choose one over another.

Aperture: how much, and how much is in focus

Aperture is the opening in your lens, measured in f-stops. A small number like f/1.8 is a wide opening: lots of light, and a shallow slice of the scene in focus. A bigger number like f/11 is a narrow opening: less light, and more of the scene sharp from front to back.

So aperture is your depth-of-field control. A portrait at f/2 melts the background into soft color. A cliff-top landscape at f/9 keeps the foreground flowers and the distant surf crisp together. Brightness is almost a bonus.

Shutter speed: how long, and what happens to motion

Shutter speed is how long the sensor collects light. Fast speeds such as 1/1000 freeze a pelican mid-dive. Slow speeds such as 1/4 turn a wave into silk, but only if the camera is on something solid.

Its side effect is motion. Fast freezes, slow blurs. There's also an unintentional version: if your shutter is too slow for your hands, the whole frame goes soft from camera shake.

ISO: how much you amplify

ISO doesn't gather more light. It brightens the signal after the fact. Low values like ISO 100 give the cleanest image. High values like ISO 3200 let you shoot in dim light, but at the cost of visible noise, that grainy speckle in the shadows.

Because the price is image quality rather than creative look, I treat ISO as the last dial I raise, not the first.

How the trade-offs play out

Here's where the triangle becomes useful. Say your meter says a scene is correctly exposed at f/4, 1/250, ISO 400. Now suppose you want more background blur. Open up to f/2, which lets in four times as much light. To keep the brightness the same, you can speed the shutter up to 1/1000. Same exposure, different look.

The reverse works too. Stop down to f/8 for a sharper landscape and you've lost light, so you can slow the shutter or raise the ISO to compensate. Every choice has a matching payment somewhere else. A few quick rules of thumb:

  • Want blur in the background? Open the aperture, then speed up the shutter or lower ISO to compensate.
  • Want to freeze action? Pick the shutter speed first, then open the aperture or raise ISO to afford it.
  • Want maximum quality? Keep ISO low and let aperture and shutter do the work.
  • Want to shoot in the dark? Something has to give: a wider aperture, a slower shutter, or a higher ISO.

Stops: the shared language

You'll hear photographers talk about "stops." One stop is a doubling or halving of light. Going from 1/250 to 1/125 is one stop more light. Going from f/4 to f/2.8 is one stop more light. Going from ISO 400 to ISO 800 is one stop more. Because they're all measured in the same unit, you can trade them one for one, and that's what makes the triangle work in your head instead of on a spreadsheet.

You don't need to memorize the full scale. Just remember that each click on the dial is roughly a doubling or halving, and you can offset a change on one side with an equal change on another.

A real example from the coast

Last winter I was photographing sea lions near the wharf in overcast light. I wanted the animals sharp and the busy background soft, so I chose f/5.6. They move constantly, so I needed at least 1/500. The light was flat, and those two choices left me under-exposed, so I raised ISO to 800. Nothing about that was complicated; it was just deciding what mattered most, then letting the other two dials absorb the cost.

Start with the priority, not the numbers

Before you touch anything, ask one question: what does this picture need most? If it's background blur, start with aperture. If it's frozen motion or silky water, start with shutter speed. If it's a clean, noise-free file, start with ISO. Set your priority, then adjust the others until the exposure looks right.

That's the entire triangle. Once it clicks, camera menus stop being intimidating and every setting becomes a creative choice. If you want to see these ideas applied to real scenes, browse my photo gallery and try to guess the settings behind each frame.
