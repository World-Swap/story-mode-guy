If you keep asking yourself why your video audio sounds bad, you're in good company. I've shot video around Santa Cruz for years, and the single most common complaint I hear is some version of "the picture looks great, but the sound is awful." The good news is that bad audio almost always comes down to three things: how far the mic is from the voice, the room you're recording in, and your levels. Fix those and you'll sound dramatically better without buying anything fancy.

Why your video audio sounds bad: distance is the big one

The microphone built into your camera or phone is usually six to ten feet from your subject. At that distance it records the person's voice and the entire room at nearly the same volume. That's where the echo, the hollow quality, and the "recorded in a bathroom" feel come from.

The fix is physics, not gear. Sound drops off fast with distance, so every time you halve the distance between mic and mouth, the voice gets much louder compared to the room. A mic about 6 to 12 inches from the speaker's mouth, just out of frame, will beat a thousand-dollar camera mic sitting across the room.

  • Lavalier (lav) mic: clips to the chest, about a hand-width below the chin. Great for interviews and talking-head video.
  • Shotgun mic on a boom: held just above the frame, angled at the mouth. Best when you can't have a mic visible.
  • Wireless kits: a lav plus a transmitter lets the speaker walk and talk without a cable.

Fix echo by fixing the room

Hard, bare surfaces bounce sound around. Tile, glass, concrete, and big empty walls are the worst offenders. When I'm setting up an interview, I clap once in the space. If I hear a sharp ring or flutter afterward, I know I have a problem.

You don't need studio foam. You need soft things:

  1. Move to a smaller room with carpet, a couch, or curtains.
  2. Hang a heavy blanket or moving pad just behind the camera, out of frame.
  3. Put the speaker so that their back is to a soft surface, like bookshelves or drapes, instead of a bare wall.
  4. Close windows and turn off fans, fridges, and air conditioning. The mic hears them even when you've stopped noticing.

Living near the coast adds its own traps: wind, surf, and the occasional leaf blower next door. If you're outdoors, put the speaker with their back to the wind and use a furry windscreen on the mic. Foam alone won't stop real wind.

Get your levels right

Levels are where a lot of otherwise decent recordings die. Too quiet and you'll boost the hiss later. Too loud and you get distortion that can never be repaired.

  • Aim for the speaker's normal talking voice to peak around -12 dB on your meter, with the loudest laughs or emphasis staying under -6 dB.
  • Never let the meter touch 0 dB. Once it clips, that sound is gone.
  • Set the level while the person is actually talking at the energy they'll use, not during a quiet "testing, one two."
  • If your camera has automatic gain, turn it off. Auto gain pumps the background noise up in every pause, which is the "hissy breathing" sound you hear in cheap videos.

Wear headphones while you record. This is the single habit that separates people who get clean audio from people who find out after the fact. If you can hear a buzz, a hum, or a clipped word in real time, you can fix it on the spot.

Quick fixes in editing

Editing can rescue some problems but not all. Keep expectations realistic: you can reduce steady noise, trim rumble, and even out volume. You cannot remove room echo or fully repair distortion.

  • High-pass filter: cut everything below about 80 Hz on voice to remove rumble from handling, traffic, and air conditioning.
  • Noise reduction: use it gently. Heavy settings make voices sound watery and robotic.
  • Compression and normalizing: bring dialogue to a consistent loudness. For online video, aim for roughly -14 to -16 LUFS overall.
  • Music under speech: keep it well below the voice, typically 15 to 20 dB lower.

A simple pre-roll checklist

Before I hit record on any shoot, I run through the same short list. Is the mic close? Is the room quiet and soft? Is auto gain off? Are my peaks around -12 dB? Am I wearing headphones? Did I record ten seconds of "room tone," just silence, so I can clean up edits later? Nine times out of ten, a problem gets caught right there.
