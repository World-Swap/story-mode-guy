I shoot with big cameras for a living, and I still pull out my phone every single day. The best iphone photography tips have nothing to do with buying gear. Your iPhone already has a remarkable sensor and lens system; most people just never change a few default habits. Here are the seven things I'd tell a friend standing next to me on West Cliff Drive.

1. Chase the light, not the subject

Light does about 80 percent of the work in any photo. Phone sensors are small, so they love soft, directional light and struggle in harsh noon sun. Shoot in the first hour after sunrise or the last hour before sunset, and when you can't, find open shade. Here on the coast, fog is a free giant softbox: a gray Santa Cruz morning makes skin and cliffs look beautiful.

2. Turn on the grid and use it

Go to Settings, then Camera, then switch on Grid. Those nine squares do two jobs. First, they keep your horizons level, which is the most common giveaway of an amateur shot. Second, they help you place your subject on a line or intersection instead of dead center. A lone surfer or a lighthouse sitting on a third almost always feels more intentional.

3. Tap to focus, then lock it

Tap your subject and a yellow box appears with a little sun icon. Press and hold until you see "AE/AF Lock." Now your focus and exposure won't hunt around when something moves in the frame. Slide your finger up or down beside the sun icon to brighten or darken the shot before you press the shutter. I pull exposure down a notch on bright scenes so the sky keeps its color.

4. Get close and change your angle

Instead of pinching to zoom digitally, which just crops and softens the image, walk closer or use the real telephoto lens if your model has one. Then change your height. Crouch to the tide pools, or shoot up at the wharf pilings. Eye-level shots are what everyone else is taking, and a lower angle is usually the quickest way to make an ordinary scene feel fresh.

5. Wipe the lens

This sounds silly, and it matters more than any setting here. Your lens lives in pockets and touches your face. A smudge gives you hazy, glowing highlights that people blame on the phone. Wipe it with a soft cloth or your shirt before every session.

6. Shoot in a way that gives you room to edit

A few habits protect your options later:

  • Use Portrait mode for people and close-ups, but keep your subject a few feet from the background so the blur looks natural.
  • Turn off the flash. Use night mode or steady your elbows against your body or a railing instead.
  • Take several frames of anything that moves. Waves, kids, and dogs are never right on the first try.
  • If your phone supports it, use ProRAW for important shots, and standard HEIC for everything else to save storage.

7. Edit gently, in the right order

You don't need a paid app. The built-in Photos editor is plenty. Work in this order and stop earlier than you think you should:

  1. Crop and straighten first, using the grid lines you already trust.
  2. Exposure, then Highlights down and Shadows up a little to recover detail.
  3. Contrast and Brilliance in small amounts, usually under 20.
  4. Warmth to fix any color cast. Sunsets can handle warm, but skin and concrete usually can't.
  5. Saturation last, and barely. If the edit is obvious, it's too much.

A good test: set the original next to your edit. If you can tell right away which one is edited, back off by a third.

Practice with one subject for a week

Pick one thing near you, like the same stretch of beach, your kitchen window, or your dog, and photograph it daily at different times. You'll learn how light changes a scene faster than any article can teach you. Apply the seven tips above and keep only the best frame each day.
