Real Estate Photography Tips for Bright, Sellable Interiors

Story Mode Guy · September 12, 2026

A buyer scrolling a listing decides in about two seconds whether to tap or keep going. A guest browsing Airbnbs does the same. Dark rooms, tilted walls, and blown-out windows lose that moment before anyone reads a word of the description. The good news is that bright, sellable interiors come from a repeatable method, not from expensive gear.

Here are the real estate photography tips that make the biggest difference, in the order I'd learn them.

1. Use a wide lens, but not the widest setting

Rooms are small and you're usually standing in a doorway, so you need a wide-angle lens. On a full-frame camera, that means roughly 16 to 24mm. On a crop-sensor body, look at 10 to 16mm lenses.

The mistake is shooting everything at the widest end. Ultra-wide framing stretches the edges, bows straight lines, and makes a bedroom look like a ballroom. Buyers notice when the room in person doesn't match the photo, and agents notice too. Stay around 18 to 22mm equivalent for most rooms and save the widest setting for tight spaces like bathrooms.

2. Get a tripod and keep it level

A tripod isn't a convenience here. It's what makes proper interior lighting possible. On a tripod you can shoot at ISO 100 for clean files, use slower shutter speeds to gather window and lamp light, and take several identical frames to blend later.

Set the camera at roughly chest height, around 48 to 55 inches. Too high and you shrink the room and show too much floor. Too low and the foreground balloons. Use a 2-second timer or remote so the shutter press doesn't shake the shot.

3. Keep your verticals straight

Crooked verticals are the fastest tell of an amateur photo. When you tilt a wide lens up or down, door frames and wall corners lean inward or outward and the room looks like it's falling over.

  • Turn on your camera's digital level and keep the camera level side to side.
  • Keep the camera pointed straight ahead rather than tilted. If you need more ceiling or floor, raise or lower the tripod instead of tilting.
  • Finish the job in editing with a lens-correction profile and a vertical straightening tool.

4. Shoot from a corner

Pointing the camera at one flat wall makes a room look shallow. Back into a corner and capture two walls, and the space gains depth and shows how it flows. Aim across the room toward the opposite corner, and avoid shooting out through doorways, which pulls the eye away from the room and often reveals mess in the next one.

5. Prep the space before you shoot

Half the job happens before the camera comes out. A cluttered room photographed perfectly is still a cluttered room. Do a walkthrough first:

  • Clear kitchen counters and bathroom surfaces almost completely.
  • Hide trash cans, pet bowls, laundry, and cords. Unplug chargers.
  • Make beds tight, square the pillows, straighten rugs.
  • Close toilet lids. It is the most forgotten shot-ruiner there is.
  • Open every blind and turn on every light, including lamps and under-cabinet lights.

A good test: would this room pass as a hotel room a guest just checked into? Clean, symmetrical, and impersonal is the target.

6. Nail a bright, even exposure

Interior settings are stable because the scene doesn't move. A good baseline is f/8, ISO 100, shot in RAW, in Aperture Priority with a 2-second timer. Let the camera pick the shutter speed. On a tripod, a slow shutter is fine.

Expose so the room looks bright and inviting, then fine-tune in editing. Buyers want light, open, cheerful spaces. If you're also trying to keep detail in the windows, that leads to the real problem in this niche.

7. Solve the window problem

Your eyes see the room and the view outside at once. A camera can't. Expose for the room and the windows blow out to white. Expose for the windows and the room goes dark. A blown-out window is the most common giveaway of a weak interior photo. There are three ways to deal with it.

Window pull (start here)

Shoot the room slightly bright in RAW, then drag the highlights down in editing to recover window detail. It's fast, needs no extra gear, and works well when windows are a small part of the frame.

Flash and ambient blend

Expose one frame for the window so the view is correct and the room is dark. Then take a second frame from the same tripod position with a flash bounced off the ceiling to light the room. Blend the two. This gives the cleanest color and the most control, and it's why working pros carry a flash. Always bounce the flash. Pointing it straight forward creates a hotspot and harsh shadows.

HDR bracketing

Take three to five frames at different shutter speeds, about 2 stops apart, and merge them in software. Change shutter speed only, never aperture. HDR works without extra lighting gear, but it's easy to overdo. If a viewer can tell it's HDR, you've gone too far.

8. Follow a shot list

Run the same checklist on every property so you don't miss anything and you work faster:

  • Kitchen: a wide two-wall shot, a straight-on of the island or counter, and one detail like hardware or a backsplash.
  • Living room: a corner shot, a reverse angle from the opposite corner, and a feature shot of the fireplace or main window.
  • Bedrooms: a corner shot showing the bed and at least one window.
  • Bathrooms: the widest lens you have, and check the mirror so you and the tripod aren't in it.
  • Exterior: sun behind you in soft light, porch lights on, and cars and bins out of frame.

A typical listing needs 20 to 35 strong photos, not 200 mediocre ones. Cull hard and deliver the best frame of each angle.

9. Edit for natural, not dramatic

Start with geometry: lens correction, then straighten verticals. Fix white balance so whites look white, and keep it consistent across the entire set so the listing feels cohesive. Fluorescent and some LED fixtures add a green tint that's easy to miss, so add a touch of magenta if a room looks slightly sickly. Then brighten, recover the windows, and add back a little contrast.

The best listing edit is invisible. Over-processed photos make buyers distrust a listing, not love it. Once one image looks right, sync its base settings across the set and fine-tune from there. It keeps you fast and consistent.

10. Know what to charge

Agents and hosts are not paying for photos for fun. They're paying because better photos mean more clicks, more showings, and more bookings. Starter pricing in many markets lands around $150 to $250 for a standard listing and $150 to $300 for an Airbnb property, with twilight exteriors, floor plans, or short videos as add-ons. Your market will vary, so check what local photographers charge and start near the lower end while you build a portfolio and reviews. Deliver within 24 to 48 hours. Fast, reliable turnaround is often what earns the repeat business.

Start with one room

You don't need to master everything at once. Pick one room, put the camera on a tripod at chest height, level it, shoot it at f/8 and ISO 100, and pull the windows back in editing. Do that on a few rooms and you'll already be ahead of most listing photos out there.
