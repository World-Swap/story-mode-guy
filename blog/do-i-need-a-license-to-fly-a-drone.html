Do I Need a License to Fly a Drone? (US Rules, Explained Simply)

Story Mode Guy · September 5, 2026

The short answer: it depends on why you're flying, not on how nice your drone is. In the US, a hobbyist and a paid pilot follow two different sets of rules, and most of the confusion people have about "needing a license" comes from mixing those two up.

Quick note before anything else. This post is general educational information about the US system, not legal advice, and drone rules change. Before you fly, check the current requirements directly with the FAA at faa.gov. If anything here disagrees with what the FAA says today, the FAA wins.

The question that decides everything: are you flying for fun or for work?

Under the FAA's framework, the purpose of the flight sets your rulebook. Not the drone's size, not the quality of the footage.

  • Recreational: flying purely for personal enjoyment. Practicing, shooting photos you'll never sell or use for a business.
  • Commercial: flying for pay, or in furtherance of a business. Real estate shots, a wedding, a roof inspection, a monetized YouTube video, a sponsored post.

The "furtherance of a business" part catches people. Shooting a friend's shop opening for free can still count, because the footage serves a business. If a flight is doing a job, treat it as commercial.

Flying for fun: no pilot's license, but there are requirements

You don't need a pilot's license to fly a drone recreationally in the US. What you do need:

  1. The TRUST test. TRUST is the Recreational UAS Safety Test. It's free, online, and run through FAA-approved providers. It's built so you can't really fail: miss a question and it teaches you the answer. You get a completion certificate, and you should keep a copy on your phone because you're expected to be able to show it.
  2. Registration, depending on weight. The number to know is 250 grams (about 0.55 lb). Drones at or above that generally need to be registered with the FAA before flying outdoors. Many popular mini drones are built to land just under that line. Weigh yours the way you actually fly it, with the battery and any accessories attached, because a prop guard can push a drone over the threshold.
  3. Remote ID, if your drone requires it. Think of Remote ID as a digital license plate: the drone broadcasts its identity and location while it's in the air. Newer registered drones often have it built in. Older ones may need an add-on module. Check your specific model.

Recreational flying also comes with ground rules: keep the drone in your visual line of sight, stay at or below 400 feet above ground level in uncontrolled airspace, give way to crewed aircraft, and get authorization before flying in controlled airspace.

Flying for money: this is where Part 107 comes in

The moment a flight is commercial, TRUST isn't enough. You need the Remote Pilot Certificate under FAA Part 107. This is the closest thing to a "drone license," and it's the one people usually mean when they ask the question.

To get it, you pass a proctored aeronautical knowledge exam at an approved testing center. The subjects are airspace and sectional charts, weather, regulations, and flight operations. Most people find chart reading the hardest part simply because it's new. You also have to be at least 16. The certificate has to be kept current through the FAA's recurrent training, so check the current interval and requirements on faa.gov rather than trusting a blog post, including this one.

One detail worth knowing: the under-250g registration break is for recreational flying. Fly any drone commercially, even a tiny one, and it generally needs to be registered.

Airspace is the part that gets people in real trouble

You can have every certificate and still fly illegally if you launch in the wrong place. A rough breakdown:

  • Uncontrolled (Class G): most rural and suburban airspace away from airports. You can generally fly up to 400 feet without asking permission, while following the other rules.
  • Controlled (Class B, C, D, E): airspace around airports. You need authorization before you fly, and near major airports it may not be available at all.

Authorization usually comes through LAANC, which gives near-instant approvals through FAA-approved apps up to a set altitude shown on a grid over each airport. If the grid says 100 feet, you can't auto-approve 200.

Don't assume from the ground. A small regional airport can put controlled airspace over a quiet town while a dense city block sits in open Class G. Also watch for Temporary Flight Restrictions, which pop up over wildfires, stadium events, and VIP movements and can appear over your usual spot with no warning. Check an official or FAA-approved airspace app at the launch spot, the day you fly.

Federal rules aren't the whole picture

The FAA governs the airspace. Cities, states, and park agencies can regulate where you take off and land. A city park can ban launches even if the sky above it is open. National parks broadly prohibit launching and landing. Here in Santa Cruz, coastal land is a patchwork of state beaches, city parks, and private property, so I check the ground rules as carefully as the airspace. Legal airspace and a legal launch spot are two separate checks.

A short pre-flight check

  1. Right credential for the purpose: TRUST for fun, Part 107 for anything paid.
  2. Drone registered if it needs to be (250g and up, or any commercial use), with the number marked on the outside.
  3. Remote ID sorted if your drone requires it.
  4. Airspace checked for your exact spot, today, plus any active TFRs.
  5. Takeoff and landing allowed at that location.
  6. Drone in your line of sight, under 400 feet, away from people and moving vehicles.
