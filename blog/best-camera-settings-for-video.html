The Best Camera Settings for Video (Frame Rate, Shutter & More)

Story Mode Guy · October 18, 2026

The best camera settings for video aren't a mystery, but most beginners get at least one of them wrong, and it's usually the one that makes footage look like a home movie instead of a film. Here's the plain-English starting point I use: pick the right frame rate, set your shutter speed by rule instead of by guess, shoot at a sensible resolution, and choose a picture profile that fits how much editing you want to do.

Frame rate for video: 24, 30, or 60 fps

Frame rate is how many pictures per second your camera records. It sets the feel of the motion more than anything else.

  • 24 fps: the traditional film cadence. Slight motion blur and a calm, dreamlike rhythm. My default for stories, travel pieces, and anything meant to feel cinematic.
  • 30 fps: a touch smoother and more "video-like." Common for interviews, vlogs, and news-style content.
  • 60 fps: very smooth, and the main reason to use it is slow motion. Shot at 60 and played back at 24 or 30, it gives you a clean, usable slow-mo.

The rule that matters most: choose a frame rate for the project and stick with it. Mixing 24 and 30 in one edit causes stutter. If you're delivering to YouTube or Instagram, either is fine. Just be consistent.

The shutter speed rule behind the "video look"

This is the setting that quietly ruins a lot of footage. Set your shutter speed to roughly double your frame rate. At 24 fps, that means 1/48, and since most cameras don't offer that exact number, 1/50 is the standard. At 30 fps, use 1/60. At 60 fps, use 1/120.

This is called the 180-degree rule. It gives each frame just enough motion blur to look natural to the eye. Go much faster, say 1/500, and motion turns crisp and choppy, like a sports broadcast or a security camera. Go much slower and everything smears.

The obvious catch is brightness. A fixed shutter speed means you can't use it to control exposure on a sunny day. Which brings us to the thing most people miss:

  • Keep ISO as low as your camera's base setting allows in bright light.
  • Control the aperture for depth of field and some exposure.
  • Use a variable ND filter to cut light so you can hold 1/50 in full sun without stopping down to f/16.

Anywhere near the Santa Cruz coast on a bright afternoon, an ND filter is basically mandatory.

Resolution: 1080p vs 4K

4K gives you more detail and room to crop or reframe in editing. It also means larger files and more demanding computers. My general rule is to record in 4K if the camera and storage can handle it, and deliver in whatever the platform needs. If your device overheats or you run out of storage, 1080p at a higher bitrate still looks excellent for most web video.

Picture profiles: standard or flat?

A picture profile controls contrast, saturation, and tone. Pick based on how much time you want to spend afterward.

  1. Standard or natural: looks good right out of the camera. Best for quick turnaround and beginners.
  2. Flat or log profiles: preserve more highlight and shadow detail but look washed out until you grade them. Great for flexibility, but they demand good exposure and some post-production work.

If you've never color graded, start with standard. A flat profile with no grading just looks worse, not better.

Other settings worth checking

  • White balance: set it manually (around 5600K for daylight) instead of leaving it on auto, so color doesn't drift mid-shot.
  • Focus: use manual focus or a locked focus point for interviews. Continuous autofocus can hunt at the worst moments.
  • Exposure: use zebras or a histogram to protect your highlights. Slightly underexposing is easier to fix than blown-out whites.
  • Bitrate and codec: use the highest quality your cards can handle if you plan to edit heavily.

My starting recipe

If I'm handed a camera and told to just start shooting, this is what I set: 4K, 24 fps, shutter 1/50, ISO at base, ND filter to hit exposure, manual white balance, and a standard or lightly flat profile. Adjust from there for the scene, not the other way around.
