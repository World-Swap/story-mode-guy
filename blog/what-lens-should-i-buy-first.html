What Lens Should You Buy First? (Prime vs Zoom, Plain English)

Story Mode Guy · July 28, 2026

"What lens should I buy first?" is the question I hear most from people who've outgrown the kit lens that came with their camera. The honest answer depends on what you shoot, not on what's trendy. I've carried a lot of glass around the California coast, and here's the plain-English version of how I'd choose your next lens.

Start With What You Actually Shoot

Look at your last 100 photos. What focal lengths did you use? Most cameras record this in the file data, and any photo library can sort by it. If most of your keepers sit at the wide end of your kit zoom, buy something wide. If you're always at the long end, you want reach. This one exercise beats any gear review.

Focal Length in Plain English

Focal length, measured in millimeters, is how much of the scene you capture. These numbers are for full frame. On an APS-C camera, multiply by about 1.5 to get the equivalent field of view.

  • 14 to 24mm (ultra-wide): Landscapes, architecture, tight interiors. Expect dramatic perspective and stretched edges.
  • 24 to 35mm (wide): Environmental scenes, street photography, travel. This is a versatile range for storytelling.
  • 50mm (normal): Close to how we see. Great for everyday shooting, portraits with some context and low-light work.
  • 85 to 135mm (short telephoto): Portraits. It flatters faces and separates your subject from the background.
  • 70 to 200mm and beyond (telephoto): Sports, wildlife, surf, distant details. It compresses space and brings far things close.

Prime vs Zoom: The Real Differences

A prime lens has one fixed focal length. A zoom covers a range. Neither is better, but they're good at different things.

Primes are typically smaller, lighter and cheaper for a given quality level. They often have wider maximum apertures like f/1.8 or f/1.4, which means more light reaches the sensor in dim rooms. They also force you to move your feet, which makes you think harder about composition.

Zooms give you flexibility. At a whale watch or a kid's birthday party, you can't walk closer, so a zoom lets you reframe instantly. A constant f/2.8 zoom is a workhorse for professionals, though it's bigger, heavier and pricier.

My rule of thumb: if you shoot unpredictable subjects, buy a zoom. If you shoot slower, deliberate subjects or you mostly want better low-light performance and image quality per dollar, buy a prime.

My Recommendations by Type of Photography

  1. General everyday and travel: A 35mm or 50mm f/1.8 prime. Inexpensive, sharp and light enough to carry everywhere.
  2. Portraits: An 85mm f/1.8 prime, or a 50mm if your budget is tight.
  3. Landscapes: A 16-35mm or 17-40mm zoom, or a 24mm prime if you prefer to keep things simple.
  4. Wildlife and sports: A 70-300mm zoom to start. Longer glass gets expensive fast.
  5. Coastal and surf: A 70-200mm covers waves, birds and cliffs from a safe distance.

If you can only buy one lens, the "nifty fifty" 50mm f/1.8 is the best value in photography. It's usually under a few hundred dollars and will teach you more than a pricey zoom.

What Matters More Than the Spec Sheet

Gear forums obsess over sharpness charts. In real use, other factors matter more.

  • Weight and size. The best lens is the one you actually bring. A heavy zoom that stays home takes no pictures.
  • Autofocus speed. Check reviews for how well it tracks moving subjects on your specific camera body.
  • Lens mount. Buy lenses that fit your camera. Third-party options from Sigma, Tamron and Viltrox can save real money, but confirm compatibility first.
  • Stabilization. If your camera body lacks it, a lens with image stabilization helps at slower shutter speeds.
  • Used glass. Good used lenses lose little value and often cost half as much as new. Buy from reputable sellers with return policies.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

  • Buying the longest zoom you can. Superzooms are convenient but soft at the extremes and slow in low light.
  • Collecting lenses too early. Learn one lens thoroughly before you add another.
  • Ignoring your body's limits. A fast lens doesn't fix a camera that struggles in low light. Weigh body and lens together.
  • Chasing the biggest aperture number. A f/1.2 lens costs a lot more than an f/1.8. Most beginners never need the difference.
