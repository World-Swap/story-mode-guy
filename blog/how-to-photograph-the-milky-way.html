How to Photograph the Milky Way With the Camera You Already Own

Story Mode Guy · November 3, 2026

You do not need a telescope, a tracking mount, or a five-figure lens to photograph the Milky Way. The most common "galaxy" shot you see online is a wide-angle nightscape: a landscape foreground under a star-filled sky, shot on an ordinary camera, on a tripod, in a single exposure of 15 to 25 seconds. If you can shoot in manual mode, you can do this on your first serious night out.

What separates a keeper from a black rectangle isn't gear. It's four things: picking the right night, picking a dark enough place, getting focus exactly right, and dialing a handful of settings. Here's how I do each one.

What you actually need

The kit list is short:

  • A camera with full manual control and RAW. Almost any DSLR or mirrorless body from the last fifteen years works. Full-frame is cleaner in the dark, but crop-sensor cameras shoot great Milky Way images too.
  • A fast, wide lens. Think f/2.8 or wider, and 14-24mm on full frame. A 14mm f/2.8 or 20mm f/1.8 is close to ideal. Your kit zoom at f/3.5-5.6 will work, you'll just push ISO harder.
  • A sturdy tripod. You're shooting 15-25 second exposures. A flimsy tripod quietly softens every frame and you'll blame the lens.
  • A headlamp with a red mode. Night vision takes 20-30 minutes to build and half a second of white light to lose.

Skip the star tracker, the telescope, and the expensive filters for now. A modest camera under a truly dark sky beats a flagship under city glow every time.

Plan the night: three things have to line up

In daylight you can improvise. At night you can't, and good conditions are rare. You need the galactic core up, the moon down, and clear skies. Miss any one and the shot isn't there.

  • The core has to be above the horizon. The bright, dusty heart of the galaxy, toward Sagittarius, is visible in the northern hemisphere roughly February through October, best in summer. Early in the season it's low in the southeast before dawn; mid-summer it crosses the southern sky most of the night; late season it sits in the southwest in the evening. In deep winter it's below the horizon during darkness.
  • The moon has to be gone. Even a half moon washes out the faint structure. Shoot the week around the new moon, or in the window after the moon has set or before it rises.
  • The sky has to be dark. This is where the Bortle scale comes in.

The Bortle scale, in one paragraph

The Bortle scale ranks sky darkness from Class 1 (pristine wilderness) to Class 9 (inner city). Bortle 1-2 gives you a spectacular, naked-eye core. Bortle 3-4 still photographs very well. Bortle 5 is marginal, with a faint core you'll have to work for in editing. Bortle 6 and up, the core is essentially lost to glow. Aim for Bortle 4 or darker. Pull up a light-pollution map and find the nearest dark zone. For a lot of people that's a one-to-two-hour drive, and it's worth it.

Check an astronomy cloud-cover forecast, not just the general weather. "20% cloud" is a ruined night if that 20% is sitting on your target. And scout your foreground in daylight or at sunset, because you can't compose in the dark what you never found in the light. PhotoPills and Stellarium will show you exactly where the core rises from your spot on your date.

Focus on a star, manually

This is the skill that trips up nearly every beginner. Autofocus has nothing to grab in a dim star field, so you focus manually, and at f/2.8 the sharp zone at infinity is unforgiving. A hair off and every star is a soft blob.

  1. Switch both lens and camera to manual focus.
  2. Open the aperture wide and temporarily raise ISO (around 12800) so live view is bright enough to see stars.
  3. Aim at the brightest star or planet you can find.
  4. Magnify live view to 100% on that point of light.
  5. Turn the focus ring slowly until the star shrinks to the smallest pinpoint. Go past it in both directions to confirm you found the true minimum.
  6. Don't trust the infinity mark on the barrel. Most lenses focus past true infinity.
  7. Drop ISO back down, and tape the focus ring if it's loose.

No bright star? A distant light hundreds of yards away is effectively at infinity. Focus on that, then swing back to your composition without touching the ring. Then take a test frame and zoom in on the stars. Recheck any time you change lenses or bump the ring.

The settings that get you a usable first frame

The whole game is gathering as much light as possible before the stars start to trail. Start here:

  • Aperture: wide open, or a third to two thirds of a stop in if the corners show coma (comet-shaped stars). f/1.8 to f/2.2 is a common nudge.
  • ISO: 3200-6400. Don't fear 6400. A bright, noisy frame you can clean up beats a dark, empty one.
  • Shutter: set by the 500 rule (below).
  • White balance: manual, around 3900-4200K, so previews stay consistent frame to frame.
  • Format: RAW. Not optional.

The 500 rule

The Earth rotates, so stars drift across your sensor. Expose too long and pinpoints become streaks. The classic guideline: maximum shutter in seconds = 500 divided by focal length. A 20mm lens on full frame gives 500 / 20 = 25 seconds. A 14mm gives about 35.

Two corrections. On crop sensors, use 300 instead of 500 (a 20mm lens on a 1.5x body gives roughly 15 seconds, not 25). And on high-resolution sensors the 500 rule is generous, so if you pixel-peep and see trailing, shorten the exposure toward 300 or even 200 divided by focal length and raise ISO to compensate.

A reliable starting recipe for a 20-24mm lens under Bortle 3-4: f/2.8, 20 seconds, ISO 3200, 4000K, RAW. Check the histogram, not the rear screen. The LCD looks blindingly bright in the dark and tricks you into underexposing. You want the histogram peak left of center but clear of the left wall. If it's dark, go to ISO 6400.

Use the 2-second self-timer or a remote so the shutter press doesn't shake a 20-second exposure.

Make it a photograph, not a screensaver

Stars alone are a wallpaper. Stars over something are a story. Put a strong silhouette in the frame: a lone tree, a ridgeline, a barn, still water that doubles the sky. Detail collapses to shape at night, so pick foregrounds that read clearly as a silhouette. Keep the horizon low, and put the core on a third line instead of dead center. Shoot one horizontal frame and one vertical with the core rising out of your foreground, and compare them at home. If you want to see how I frame these, browse the gallery.

Clean it up: stack and edit

High ISO means noise, and the free fix is stacking. Shoot 8-15 identical frames back to back without touching the camera, then feed them to sky-stacking software like Sequator (Windows) or Starry Landscape Stacker (Mac). It aligns the stars and averages the frames, which keeps the real signal and cancels the random noise. Ten stacked frames at ISO 6400 can look cleaner than a single frame at ISO 1600.

In editing, a RAW file will look flat and gray at first. That's normal. Fix white balance first, add contrast, use Dehaze gently, boost the core with a local mask instead of the whole sky, and apply noise reduction last, stopping before the small stars start to vanish. If a slider looks great, back it off ten percent.

Your first-night checklist

  1. Confirm core up, moon down, clear forecast for your window.
  2. Arrive at dusk and set your composition while you can still see.
  3. Let your eyes dark-adapt and use only red light.
  4. Manual mode, RAW, wide aperture, ISO 3200, shutter from the 500 or 300 rule, WB around 4000K.
  5. Focus on a bright star at 100% magnification, then lock it.
  6. Test frame: check the histogram and zoom in on the stars.
  7. Fire a burst of 8-15 frames on the 2-second timer.
  8. Recheck focus and wipe dew every 20-30 minutes.
