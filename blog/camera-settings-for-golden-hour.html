The Best Camera Settings for Golden Hour (a Simple Starting Point)

Story Mode Guy · August 1, 2026

Golden hour is the easiest light to fall in love with and one of the trickiest to expose, because it's changing the entire time you're shooting. The settings that were perfect ten minutes ago are a stop too dark now. So instead of memorizing one magic combination, it's better to start from a sensible baseline and know which dial to turn as the light drops.

Here's the starting point I use, and how I adjust from there.

The baseline: where to start

  • Mode: Aperture Priority (A / Av) if you want to keep it simple, or full Manual if you want total control. Both work — Manual just means you're the one keeping up with the changing light.
  • Aperture: f/8 to f/11 for landscapes where you want everything sharp front-to-back. Drop to f/2.8–f/4 if you want a subject to pop against a soft, glowing background.
  • ISO: Start at your camera's base — usually ISO 100. Only raise it once the light drops enough that your shutter speed gets too slow to hand-hold.
  • Shutter speed: Let it fall where the exposure needs it, but don't let it drop below 1/125s handheld, or bring a tripod.
  • White balance: This is the big one — see below.

Don't let the camera "fix" the warmth

The whole point of golden hour is the warm color. If you leave white balance on Auto, your camera will often try to neutralize it and hand you back a flat, cool image. Set your white balance to Daylight (around 5500K) or nudge it warmer to Cloudy/Shade to keep — or push — the gold. If you shoot RAW, you can adjust this later, but getting it close in camera helps you judge the scene.

Meter for the sky, then decide

Golden hour has huge contrast between the bright sky and the darkening ground. Your camera can't hold both. So decide what matters:

  • Want a colorful sky and silhouettes? Let the foreground go dark. Meter for the sky (or dial in negative exposure compensation, around −1) so the color stays rich instead of blowing out.
  • Want detail in the foreground? Expose brighter and accept a lighter sky — or shoot two frames and blend them, or use a graduated filter.

When in doubt, protect the highlights. A slightly dark foreground can be lifted in editing; a blown-out sky is gone for good. Watch your histogram and keep the right edge from slamming into the wall.

Chase the light as it moves

The magic of golden hour is that it keeps changing — and so should your settings. As the sun sinks:

  1. The light warms and dims. Your shutter speed slows to compensate. When it drops near your hand-holdable limit, that's your cue to raise ISO — one stop at a time — or put the camera on a tripod.
  2. After the sun is down, don't pack up. Blue hour arrives about 15–25 minutes later, and it's a whole second act — deep blue skies, city and ride lights coming on, glassy reflections. You'll be on a tripod by now with a slow shutter, and it's worth it.

Then finish it in editing

Even a well-exposed golden-hour RAW file usually looks a little flat straight out of camera — that's normal. A few minutes of editing brings back the warmth and depth your eye actually saw: lift the shadows, protect the highlights, warm the temperature, and add a touch of contrast. Golden hour rewards people who show up prepared and stay late. Get your baseline dialed before the color starts, protect your highlights, and let the light — and your settings — evolve together.
